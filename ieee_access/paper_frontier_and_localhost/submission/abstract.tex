Production AI now learns mostly outside the model weights, and the industry has built, piece by piece, most of an optimizer for that layer without ever assembling it. After a failure, teams edit instructions, skills, memories, tools, orchestration graphs, evaluation suites, and governance rules far faster than any weight update, and two earlier results of this series suggest why that is the right place: errors concentrate in a few key decisions that fail in a small, recurring catalogue of modes. This survey of 256 production and research systems reads that layer as one optimizer with two halves. The first traces a recurring failure to one scaffold coordinate, proposes one edit, and keeps it only if a gate shows the deployment's own failure rate falling. The second decides how far the edit may travel. What a deployment can automate is fixed by the feedback its gate receives: an exact check, an observed outcome, a judgment, or a delayed reward. An accepted edit improves only what that gate measured. Across the corpus the first half is routine and much of the second exists in parts: evidence is batched before acceptance, retirement is gated, lineage ships in products, formats and registries have converged, and the first federations of deployments exist. No surveyed system assembles those parts into promotion across organizations under versioned lineage, access control, and rollback. We give the four-property criterion that would, the failure surface left open until it exists, and, for each signal class, the piece that is missing.
