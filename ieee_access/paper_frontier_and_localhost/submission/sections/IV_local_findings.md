## What local adaptation actually closes {#sec:findings}

A system can detect a failure, explain it, and write a plausible correction without ever establishing that the correction helps. SkillForge is such a system. Its judge agrees with people more than nine times in ten and identifies replies that should be rewritten. SkillForge updates the skill from those replies but commits the updated skill without an acceptance test [@skillforge2026]. The judge's calibration concerns which replies are inadequate; it does not measure the effect of the resulting skill edits.

SkillRL leaves its corrections untested by a different route. A success-rate threshold triggers proposals for new skills, and the skill bank accepts each proposal unconditionally [@skillrl2026]. Both systems respond to experience, yet neither has the local gate defined in Section REF(sec:loop). Proposing an edit is not accepting it, so the survey records who accepts an edit separately from the edit's author and its trigger.

Among recent systems with a local loop, however, automatic acceptance dominates: of the 105 core systems most recently added to the corpus, 96 have a local loop, and 89 of those use a program to retain or reject edits. Inside these loops, evaluation is comparatively mature, while the failure record and the credit assignment that should supply the gate are often incomplete.

### Finding the right thing to change

A useful failure record must retain what went wrong in a form that supports later comparison. Production memory more often records preferences, successes, or conversation history. Only one field report in the corpus describes a store of dead ends intended to prevent their recurrence, and that evidence comes from a single self-reported deployment [@helwig2026memory]. The persistent store may therefore be extensive while the record that failure-driven adaptation needs is missing.

Even when failures are retained, nearly every system uses them in arrival order, letting the most recent session determine the next update. Two systems weight that experience instead: Task-CoEvolve through variance-weighted task sampling and AutoSaddler through mini-batch failure signals [@miyai2026taskcoevolve; @park2026autosaddler]. Prioritizing by both recurrence and severity, which the local loop calls for, remains uncommon in the surveyed implementations.

The next step, credit assignment, is harder, because a failed output does not reveal whether its cause was an instruction, a retrieved memory, a skill, or the route through a group of agents. This attribution step is what harness-optimization papers repeatedly identify as the bottleneck [@lin2026ahe; @zehle2026cantante; @lee2026metaharness; @nguyen2026harnessevo; @liu2026skillsvote]. An evaluation of harness optimizers reaches the same conclusion: by downstream score alone, a harness optimizer cannot be told from trial and error [@ong2026harnesseval].

Existing attribution methods usually operate within one substrate. Meta-Harness preserves candidate harnesses and the record of which edits helped [@lee2026metaharness]. SkillsVote attributes outcomes to the skill, exploration, or environment before admitting an update [@liu2026skillsvote]. SkillOpt-Lite keeps only the attributes that recur across rollouts, and its ablation finds this consensus mechanism load-bearing [@shen2026skilloptlite]. These methods give the proposer better evidence, but none solves credit assignment across all six scaffold substrates.

Research systems commonly generate candidates by reflecting on trajectories. Where proposal instead waits on a person noticing a problem, improvement remains intermittent even when evaluation runs automatically. In production prompt platforms, an engineer writes the candidate, and session feedback never triggers one. Public skill repositories depend on contributors: a field study of five such repositories finds that every substantive edit was committed under a named human account and merged by a person, most carrying an AI co-author trailer [@shen2026whomaintains].

### Testing the candidate

Skill systems differ in where the gate sits, and some have none. SAGE and COSPLAY close both the local and the promotion loop inside a research environment [@wang2025sage; @wu2026cosplay]. SkillChain adds a reflection gate after its judge, at which a person decides which refined skills are deployed [@hu2026skillchain]. SkillRL and SkillForge, described above, leave proposed edits unchecked.

Orchestration has moved from score-guided search toward gated edits. Early systems searched for agent topologies under an evaluation score [@zhuge2024gptswarm; @zhang2024aflow]. More recent systems evaluate proposed topology changes before keeping them; TOCOMAS also requires a structural invariant [@chu2026agentgrad; @chen2026harnessforge; @zhao2026tocomas]. OptiMAS is an exception: its optimizer reads trajectories and validation scores to write the next configuration, but the score informs the edit rather than supplying a keep-if-better decision [@cheng2026optimas]. No orchestration system closes both loops with lineage on the promoted artifact.

For instruction files, the most widely deployed artifact, prevalence does not settle effectiveness. They are present across Claude Code, Cursor, AGENTS.md [@agentsmd2025], Windsurf, and Continue.dev, and adoption studies find them in roughly a fifth to a quarter of coding-agent projects [@robbes2026agenticmuch; @galster2026harnessconfig]. One controlled study reports higher inference cost without improved task success [@gloaguen2026agentsmd]. Another toggles the root AGENTS.md in paired runs on 124 pull requests and reports about 29% lower median wall-clock time and 17% fewer median output tokens, with total tokens approximately unchanged. It does not measure correctness [@lulla2026empirical]. The two studies measure different outcomes and disagree on cost, and neither establishes a general task-success benefit from accumulating instruction files.

Human local gates appear only in curation: VerificAgent's fact-checked, frozen memories [@nguyen2025verificagent] and SkillChain's reflection gate. In this classification, prompt platforms and registries without a failure-driven proposal process have no local gate. Their checks act at release or at admission to a shared pool, and both belong to promotion.

### Gathering enough evidence to accept

An acceptance test can itself be too noisy to support a decision from one run. Several newer systems address this noise, and acceptance increasingly depends on a body of evidence instead of one favorable observation. SAGE-gate, a statistical acceptance method unrelated to SAGE, uses paired per-item comparisons before committing an edit [@wang2026sagegate]. VaG requires three heterogeneous critics to pass [@shang2026vag]. AutoSaddler also uses its mini-batch failure signals in validation-based selection [@park2026autosaddler].

Two mechanisms should not be conflated: averaging scores and requiring agreement. Averaging $k$ independent noisy scores reduces the variance of their mean by a factor of $1/k$. Requiring agreement among $k$ independent checks, each of which falsely accepts with probability $p$, gives an idealized false-acceptance probability of $p^k$. Both results assume independence, which actual critics need not satisfy. Appendix REF(app:gates) distinguishes the gate families and their roles.

### Maintaining the artifacts that survive

How a new edit scores also depends on the artifacts already present. Stale or conflicting skills can cause failures that obscure the effect of the candidate under test. Ratchet reports no gain from model-written skills over a no-skill baseline until lifecycle hygiene is applied, while human-curated skills provide a double-digit gain [@zhang2026ratchet]. Eleven surveyed skill systems now treat removal as an operation that must pass a check, just as admission does [@zhang2026librarydrift; @wei2026skillsmith; @pu2026skillops; @wang2026assay] (Supplementary Material S6).

Memory products have advanced substantially on a different part of maintenance: recording an entry's history. Letta's Context Repositories use git, Mem0 retains per-memory history, and Zep represents facts bitemporally [@letta2026memfs; @mem02026history; @zep2026facts]. These records can explain what the system remembers and how that state arose.

History does not guarantee correction. A study of five production backends finds that all five still return facts that were revoked [@shen2026revoked]. The record contains the change, but retrieval does not reliably enforce it. Research systems divide between provenance and write controls [@wang2026toki; @xu2026ppmf; @margalit2026governed; @sun2026vermem] and verifier-gated failure correction [@yu2026recuris; @wang2026memguard]. Cuadros et al.'s governed collaborative memory combines provenance, versioning, and correction, and earns the top memory score [@cuadros2026governed].

Reversal has its own limits. SkillSmith and SkillAudit can roll back a single edit [@wei2026skillsmith; @gao2026skillaudit], and EvoUndo rejects edits that cannot be undone [@sah2026evoundo]. VaG finds that reversing a contaminated skill recovers little once other skills have been derived from it [@shang2026vag]. An edit's consequences may therefore outlast the edit. Section REF(sec:failure) returns to this problem for promotion and artifact descendants.

### A complete local example

SkillOpt meets all five conditions [@yang2026skillopt]. Its persistent state is one markdown skill document used by a frozen agent. A held-out exact-match split supplies the loss. Rollout and reflection batches supply the failure evidence. A consensus filter helps attribute recurring errors, and bounded add, delete, and replace operations propose edits. The gate accepts a candidate only when its score strictly improves.

SkillOpt's authors test it on six benchmarks, seven target models, and three harnesses and report it best or tied in every cell. Its follow-up, SkillOpt-Lite, keeps the held-out gate and the consensus filter but drops a rejected-edit buffer and a slow update schedule, and it matches or exceeds the original [@shen2026skilloptlite]. In that setting, the comparison supports the view that the gate and this form of credit assignment matter, and it identifies the removed controls as stabilizers, not necessities.

SkillOpt also evaluates transfer across models, harnesses, and benchmarks. Those experiments measure how far the learned artifact remains useful, which is a separate question from the acceptance decision that admits an edit to the shared skill bank. That separation becomes central once the same local procedure runs where correctness is harder to measure.
