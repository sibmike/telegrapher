## Open problems and conclusion {#sec:open}

Five open problems follow from the survey, one per feedback-signal class and one for the architecture.

1. **Exact signals: credit assignment from a failing check to a scaffold coordinate.** A test tells the loop that an output failed, not which instruction, skill, or tool binding caused it. Slot attribution [@nguyen2026harnessevo], intervention-based attribution [@chu2026agentgrad], and contrastive multi-agent attribution [@zehle2026cantante] each solve one substrate's case; a general method is the bottleneck named by every harness-optimization paper.
2. **Observable outcomes: receiving-side evaluation before promotion.** Four systems now evaluate at the receiving side and one of them still misses a fifth of its transfers; the gate that a federated scaffold optimizer needs is a held-out test at the receiving tenant, proposed but not yet built [@yang2026fao].
3. **Judged signals: calibrating a gate that is itself being edited.** Fourteen weight-side rubric-reward papers include five that co-evolve the rubric with the policy, and none of those five reports agreement with human judgment; on the scaffold side three systems report judge calibration and none calibrates a judge under edit. The reflexive gate of Section REF(sec:signal) has no measured instance.
4. **Delayed signals: telling descent from search.** Where the outcome arrives after many edits, an optimizer's gains cannot be distinguished from trial-and-error by outcome alone [@ong2026harnesseval]; evaluation of harness optimizers against their attribution claims rather than their scores is the open method.
5. **The governed cross-tenant corner.** Properties (a) through (d) of Section REF(sec:gap), with the receiving-side gate of problem 2 as the piece that would make cross-organization promotion safe in the two classes where it should be automatic.

The field has built an external adaptation layer for language models but not the optimizer that should govern it. The reason the layer belongs where it is was established by the two papers this article completes: reliability over long outputs hinges on a sparse set of key decisions, and inside a bounded deployment those decisions fail in a small, recurring catalogue of modes that a targeted budget of fixes can cover. The place to spend that budget is a persistent, inspectable, reversible artifact outside the weights, and once the loop that edits it is gated and scoped, the arrangement is not a patch on an inadequate model but the architecture that the error structure calls for. The survey shows how far practice has come toward that loop. The local loop closes automatically in most research systems and in a growing number of production ones; evidence is batched before acceptance; retirement and expiry have become first-class; lineage has reached production memory. What has not moved is the second loop. No surveyed system promotes a validated edit across an organization boundary under an automatic gate with lineage, access control, and rollback, and the signal-class analysis says this is not one gap but four, each with its own reachable answer. The frontier model generalizes. The local scaffold specializes. Reliability comes from governing that specialization, and the instruments that judge each edit are themselves part of the moving scaffold, so the same evidence-radius and lifecycle discipline the optimizer needs must extend to the gates that judge it.
