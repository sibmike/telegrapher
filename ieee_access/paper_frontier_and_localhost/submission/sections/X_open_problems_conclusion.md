## Open problems and conclusion {#sec:open}

Five problems remain, each sharper when stated as a decision a deployment must make.

**Attributing a failure to an editable cause.** No surveyed method attributes a failure to the responsible instruction, memory, skill, or tool binding across all substrates, even where an exact check establishes that the output failed. Three methods provide narrower starting points: slot attribution [@nguyen2026harnessevo], intervention-based attribution [@chu2026agentgrad], and contrastive multi-agent attribution [@zehle2026cantante]. Attribution is harder under observed outcomes, whose causes are more confounded.

**Establishing improvement at the recipient.** A transferred artifact must be evaluated against the receiving deployment's tasks and measure. Receiving-side checks show that this evaluation is feasible in selected settings, and Evo2Team's failures on unseen graphs show the limits of the checks' coverage. Federated Agent Optimization proposes but does not implement a receiving-client gate [@yang2026fao]. Connecting such checks to provenance, access control, and reversal remains the architectural problem taken up last.

**Maintaining calibration while the criterion changes.** On judged tasks, a judge must stay aligned with people across continuing revisions of its rubric. SkillCoach shows that an evolved rubric can be audited offline against human-gold references [@zhu2026skillcoach], but no surveyed scaffold system reports calibration maintained through repeated revisions in deployment. The neighboring reward literature also reports no human-agreement result for a co-evolving rubric or judge; RLPR's probability-based reward is a distinct case. A useful solution must evaluate the moving criterion as well as the outputs it scores.

**Evaluating improvement with delayed outcomes.** When many edits occur before a meaningful result arrives, no gate can accept each edit as it is made. Proxy milestones and proposal pruning may still be useful, but downstream gains alone do not establish successful attribution. Harness optimizers should therefore be assessed against both their final scores and their claims about why particular edits help [@ong2026harnesseval].

**Connecting local learning to governed sharing.** No surveyed system combines automatic local acceptance, promotion across organizations, versioned lineage with access control, and rollback, although the components exist in different systems. A system that integrates them must preserve tenant isolation, evaluate the recipient, and account for artifacts derived from a change that is later withdrawn. Where feedback cannot support automatic acceptance, such a system must retain the corresponding human decision or delayed evaluation.

The survey's central observation is that persistent changes outside the weights have become an important form of adaptation, while the evidence supporting those changes remains fragmented. Many systems can test a local candidate. Fewer can reliably explain which recurring failure it addresses. None of the public systems examined here has assembled the machinery for transferring the resulting improvement to another organization.

The frontier model generalizes. The local scaffold specializes. Reliability comes from governing that specialization: knowing what each change improved, where that evidence applies, and how the change can be reversed. Such governance must extend to the evaluations themselves when they become part of the adapting system.
