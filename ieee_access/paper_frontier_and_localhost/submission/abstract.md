Production systems built on large language models now adapt mostly outside the model weights, and this article asks what optimization discipline that adaptation needs. After a failure, teams edit instructions, skills, memories, tools, orchestration graphs, evaluation suites, and governance rules far faster than any weight update. Two earlier results of this series suggest why that layer is the right place: errors concentrate in a few key decisions that fail in a small, recurring catalogue of modes. We read the layer as one surface governed by one loop: a recurring failure is traced to a scaffold coordinate, one edit is proposed, a gate accepts or rejects it on measured loss, and a second decision sets how far the accepted edit may travel. Five conditions make this loop an optimizer rather than patchwork; the decisive one, a measurable loss, depends on the feedback signal the gate receives: an exact check (code, mathematics), an observable outcome (completed tasks), a judgment (writing, design), or a delayed reward (research). The signal's class fixes what the gate can be, which pairings of algorithmic and human gates are reachable, and which failure dominates. Across 256 production and research systems, scored on six substrates, with the scores released, the local loop is routine and the governed second loop is undocumented: no surveyed system combines automated local adaptation with promotion across organizations under versioned lineage, access control, and rollback. We map the failure surface this gap leaves open and state the open problem each signal class poses.
