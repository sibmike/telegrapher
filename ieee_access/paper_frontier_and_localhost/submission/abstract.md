Every serious AI deployment now improves itself outside the model weights. After a failure, a team edits instructions, skills, memories, tools, orchestration, or evaluation suites, and the system changes within the hour; the weights change on a cycle of months. This survey of 256 production and research systems asks what discipline that layer needs and finds most of its optimizer built and never assembled. The optimizer is one loop with two halves: trace a recurring failure to the artifact responsible, make one edit, keep it only if the deployment's failure rate falls, then decide how far it travels, to a team, an organization, or every customer. Its first half closes by program in nearly every failure-driven loop, though its trace is hardly built. The second exists in parts that no surveyed system assembles into promotion across organizations with lineage, access control, and rollback, so every customer relearns the same fixes and nobody knows which rule helped. The reason is structural. What a deployment can automate is fixed by the feedback that keeps or rejects an edit: an exact check, an observed outcome, a judgment, or a reward months late. An accepted edit improves only what that feedback measured, so no lift travels on its own. The survey turns that finding into tools: five conditions that separate an optimizer from a pile, four feedback classes that say which half a deployment can automate, four properties to audit the unassembled half against, and the piece each class should build first.
