### lightman2024lets
- Source of record: https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/aca97732e30bcf1303bc22ac3924fd16-Abstract-Conference.html (ICLR 2024, poster per the OpenReview venue field; proceedings pp. 39578-39601; no DOI). Access: full text (ICLR proceedings PDF https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/aca97732e30bcf1303bc22ac3924fd16-Paper-Conference.pdf and arXiv v1 PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20050v1; the cited numbers are identical).
- Retraction: none found. ICLR assigns no DOI and Crossref has no record of the paper; the bibliographic query's top 3 are unrelated, and the retraction-filtered query returns only unrelated retracted works. arXiv 2305.20050 has one version (v1) and no withdrawal notice.
- Card:
  - Setting: MATH. All large-scale models are fine-tuned from the base GPT-4 model (no RLHF) after extra training on about 1.5B math tokens (MathMix); one fixed generator writes newline-delimited step-by-step solutions (Sec. 2.2-2.3, pp. 2-3). Human labellers marked each step positive, negative or neutral; PRM800K holds 800K step labels on 75K solutions to 12K problems (Sec. 2.4, p.3), and step labels stop at the first incorrect step (Sec. 2.6, p.5). Because 4.5K MATH test problems went into PRM800K, evaluation uses the remaining 500 test problems, drawn uniformly at random (p.3; App. C, p.13). A reward model is scored by best-of-N: it picks the top-ranked of N uniformly sampled solutions, and the pick is graded by its final answer (Sec. 2.1, p.2).
  - What it establishes:
    - At large scale, the process-supervised reward model (PRM) beats the outcome-supervised one (ORM) and majority voting at every N, and the gap widens as N grows (Sec. 3 and Fig. 3, p.5). The two training sets differ (PRM800K versus 100 uniform samples per problem), so the authors do not treat this as a like-for-like comparison (Sec. 2, p.2; Sec. 3, p.5).
    - In a controlled small-scale comparison, where the large PRM supplies labels for identical datasets, process supervision outperforms both forms of outcome supervision at every data size (Sec. 4.1 and Fig. 4a, p.6). Active learning makes process supervision about 2.6 times more data-efficient (Sec. 4.2, p.7).
    - On recent AP and AMC problems (best-of-100) the PRM also leads (Sec. 5 and Table 1, p.7).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Fig. 3 inset table, percent solved with best-of-1860: ORM 72.4, PRM 78.2, Majority Voting 69.6 (p.5). Out-of-distribution Table 1, aggregate row (best-of-100): ORM 63.8%, PRM 72.9%, Majority Vote 61.3% (p.7; the table's problem counts sum to 234, while the text says 224).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: the large-scale ORM and PRM training sets are not directly comparable (pp. 2 and 5); the authors do not know how far the results generalize beyond math (Sec. 6.2, p.8); some test-set contamination may have slightly inflated MATH scores, though they expect it to affect all methods alike (Sec. 6.3, p.8); the generator is never trained with RL, and only the reward model is studied (Sec. 2.1, p.2).
  - Relation to the review: precedent for RQ2. The labels and the PRM locate the first wrong step in a solution, and the supervision deliberately stops at that step, treating the location of the first error as the information that matters. The large-scale result also bears on the voting analysis: at N = 1,860, majority voting trails selection by either reward model.
  - The hinge: the paper measures how often a reward model picks a correct solution from many samples. It does not measure how errors are distributed over the steps of one solution or whether they compound.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Feedback on each reasoning step (process supervision) trained a better verifier than feedback on final answers only (outcome supervision); with best-of-N selection, the process-supervised model solved 78% of a representative subset of the MATH test set."
  - Source passage: "solves 78% of problems from a representative subset of the MATH test set" (Abstract, p.1). The figure is the best-of-1860 result of the GPT-4-based PRM on the 500 held-out MATH test problems (Fig. 3, p.5; App. C, p.13). "Better verifier" is supported directly by the controlled small-scale comparison (Sec. 4.1, p.6) and, with unmatched training sets, by the large-scale one (Sec. 3, p.5).
  - Scope: MATH only; GPT-4-based generator and reward models; N = 1,860 samples per problem; human step labels.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. Recommended fuller wording: "A GPT-4-based verifier trained on human labels for each reasoning step (process supervision) picked correct solutions more reliably than one trained on final-answer correctness (outcome supervision): choosing the best of 1,860 sampled solutions, it solved 78.2% of 500 held-out MATH test problems, against 72.4% for the outcome-supervised verifier and 69.6% for majority voting [lightman2024lets]."
- Bib notes: title, author list, pages 39578-39601 and year verified against the ICLR proceedings page and its BibTeX export (https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/4100-/bibtex) and against the OpenReview record (venue "ICLR 2024 poster"). The ICLR version lists "Yuri Burda" and "Harrison Edwards"; the arXiv record lists "Yura Burda" and "Harri Edwards". The bib follows the ICLR version. Booktitle follows the house style already used for ICLR 2024 in references.bib. No DOI exists.

### cobbe2021training
- Source of record: https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168 (arXiv preprint; v2, 18 Nov 2021; I found no peer-reviewed version). Access: full text (arXiv v2 PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.14168v2).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref has no record of the title; its top 3 bibliographic matches and the retraction-filtered results are unrelated works. The arXiv abs page shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: GSM8K, 8.5K grade-school math word problems written by people, split 7.5K train and 1K test, each needing 2 to 8 steps (Sec. 2, p.3). Models are from the GPT-3 family, mainly 6B and 175B, with a calculator invoked through annotations (Sec. 4, p.6). The fine-tuning baseline is scored on one greedy (T = 0) sample (Sec. 4.1, p.6). For verification, a generator is fine-tuned for 2 epochs, 100 completions per training problem are sampled and labelled by final-answer correctness, and a verifier is trained on them; at test time the method samples 100 completions and returns the one the verifier ranks highest (Sec. 4.2, pp. 7-8).
  - What it establishes:
    - With enough training data, verification gives a large gain over fine-tuning; at small dataset sizes it does not help, which the authors attribute to overfitting to the correct answer (Sec. 4.2 and Fig. 5, p.8).
    - Token-level verifiers, which predict a value after every token, end up better than solution-level verifiers and overfit less (Sec. 4.3, p.9).
    - For the 6B verifier, searching over more completions helps up to about 400 and then hurts, as adversarial solutions start to fool it; a majority vote among the top-ranked samples adds a further gain (Sec. 5.1, pp. 10-11).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): on the full training set, 6B verification slightly outperforms a fine-tuned 175B model, a boost about equal to a 30x increase in model size (Sec. 6, p.12; also contribution 2, p.2). Fine-tuning a 6B model to output the final answer without intermediate steps drops its accuracy from 20.6% to 5.2% (Sec. 4.1, p.7).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: verification is not beneficial at low dataset sizes (p.8); a large generator with a small verifier works much better than the reverse, which suggests the verifier may rely on coarse heuristics rather than thorough checking (Sec. 4.3, p.10); 1.7% of problems still produced disagreement among contractors (App. A, p.15).
  - Relation to the review: precedent. The introduction motivates verifiers by the sensitivity of multi-step math to single mistakes: autoregressive models have no way to correct their own errors, and off-course solutions soon become unrecoverable (Sec. 1, p.1). That is the dependence intuition of RQ3, stated as motivation rather than measured. Best-of-N selection and voting among top-ranked samples are the selection settings that the review's voting analysis concerns.
  - The hinge: the comparison sets selection among 100 complete sampled solutions against one greedy sample. The paper does not locate where in a solution errors arise or measure how they depend on each other.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Cobbe et al. introduced GSM8K and showed that sampling many candidate solutions and selecting one with a trained verifier outperformed fine-tuning alone."
  - Source passage: "6B verification slightly outperforms a finetuned 175B model" (Sec. 6, p.12). The surrounding sentence ties this result to the full dataset; Fig. 5 and its text (p.8) show no benefit from verification at small training-set sizes.
  - Scope: GSM8K; GPT-3 6B and 175B; 100 candidates per problem; a training set large enough for the verifier to help.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "Cobbe et al. introduced GSM8K, 8.5K grade-school math word problems, and showed that once enough training problems were available, sampling 100 candidate solutions and returning the one a trained verifier ranked highest beat fine-tuning alone; on the full training set, a 6B verifier slightly outperformed a fine-tuned 175B model [cobbe2021training]."
- Bib notes: the twelve authors, their order and spelling (including "Lukasz Kaiser", printed without the Polish letter in both the PDF and the arXiv record), the title and the year verified on the arXiv abs page and the PDF title block. arXiv-only; entered as @misc with the arXiv DOI.

### wang2024mathshepherd
- Source of record: https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.510/ (ACL 2024, Volume 1: Long Papers, pp. 9426-9439, Bangkok; DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.510). Access: full text (ACL Anthology PDF https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.510.pdf and arXiv v3 PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.08935v3; the cited passages agree).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref record 10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.510 has no update or retraction relation; the retraction-filtered query matches nothing with this title; arXiv shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: GSM8K and MATH. Generators (LLaMA2-7B/13B/70B, LLemma-7B/34B, Mistral-7B, DeepSeek-67B) are fine-tuned on MetaMATH. Reward-model training data: 15 sampled solutions per training problem from 7B and 13B models, deduplicated, about 170K solutions for GSM8K and 270K for MATH; every step is labelled by a "completer" (LLemma-7B) that finishes the solution N = 8 times (Sec. 4, Parameter Setting, p.9430). Verifiers built on LLaMA2-70B (GSM8K) and LLemma-34B (MATH) rerank 256 sampled outputs; Mistral-7B reward models drive step-by-step PPO (p.9430; Table 1, p.9431).
  - What it establishes:
    - Two automatic step labels (Sec. 3.3.2, Eqs. 3-4, p.9429): hard estimation (HE) marks a step good if any of the N completions from it reaches the gold answer; soft estimation (SE) uses the fraction of completions that do. A step's quality is defined as its potential to reach the correct answer (Sec. 3.3.1, pp. 9428-9429).
    - The trained process reward model uses the HE labels (Sec. 4, p.9430).
    - As a verifier, Math-Shepherd beats self-consistency and an outcome reward model for every generator in Table 1 (Sec. 4.1, p.9430; Table 1, p.9431); as a reward for step-by-step PPO it improves Mistral-7B (Abstract, p.9426; Sec. 4.1).
    - Checked against 160 GSM8K steps the authors annotated by hand, HE labels reach 86% accuracy with a LLaMA2-70B completer at N = 4; accuracy falls at larger N, which the authors attribute to false positives (Sec. 5.2 and Fig. 3a, p.9432).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Mistral-7B with step-by-step PPO goes from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and from 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH, and reaches 89.1% and 43.5% when Math-Shepherd also verifies (Abstract, p.9426; Table 2, p.9431). DeepSeek-67B: 93.3% on GSM8K with Math-Shepherd, and 48.1% on MATH500 with self-consistency plus Math-Shepherd (Table 1, p.9431).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: the completion step is computationally expensive; the automatic labels are noisy; the PRM trained on these labels beats one trained on PRM800K, but PRM800K's GPT-4 outputs differ from the open models' outputs, so the effect of the label noise remains undetermined (Limitations, p.9434).
  - Relation to the review: precedent for RQ2. The label scores a step by whether rollouts from it can still reach the right answer, the same rollout logic as the forking-token and critical-token studies in the hardness section, applied to steps rather than tokens.
  - The hinge: in the trained model the label is binary and asks whether any of 8 completions succeeds. It flags steps from which success is still reachable, judged only by final answers; it is not an estimate of the per-step probability of error and does not check the step's own correctness.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Math-Shepherd labels each step by how often rollouts that continue from it reach the correct answer, and trains a step-level verifier on these labels without human annotation."
  - Source passage: "we train the PRM using the hard estimation version" (Sec. 4, Parameter Setting, p.9430). The frequency label in the intended sentence is the paper's soft estimation (Eq. 4, p.9429), which it defines and analyses (Sec. 5.2) but does not use for the trained verifier; the hard label (Eq. 3) marks a step good if any completion reaches the gold answer.
  - Scope: GSM8K and MATH; LLemma-7B completer with N = 8.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "Math-Shepherd labels each step without human annotation by having a separate model finish the solution several times from that step (eight times in the main experiments) and checking the final answers: in the version used for training, a step counts as good if any completion reaches the correct answer, and a soft variant uses the fraction that do; a step-level verifier trained on these labels outperformed self-consistency and an outcome-supervised verifier on GSM8K and MATH [wang2024mathshepherd]."
- Bib notes: verified against the ACL Anthology .bib (https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.510.bib) and the Crossref record: nine authors, title, pages 9426-9439, Bangkok, August 2024, DOI. The arXiv record abbreviates two names ("Xu, R. X.", "Wu, Y."); the bib follows the Anthology ("Xu, Runxin", "Wu, Yu").

### tyen2024llms
- Source of record: https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.826/ (Findings of ACL 2024, pp. 13894-13908, Bangkok; DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.findings-acl.826). Access: full text (ACL Anthology PDF https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.826.pdf and arXiv v3 PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.08516v3; every number cited below is identical in both).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref record 10.18653/v1/2024.findings-acl.826 has no update or retraction relation; the retraction-filtered query returns one unrelated record; arXiv (v1-v3) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: BIG-Bench Mistake, 2,186 chain-of-thought traces generated by PaLM 2 Unicorn at temperature 0 on five BIG-Bench tasks (word sorting, tracking shuffled objects, logical deduction, multistep arithmetic, Dyck languages), each step generated separately (Sec. 2, p.13895). Each trace is annotated with the location of its first logical mistake, by at least three annotators for four tasks (Krippendorff's alpha 0.979-0.998) and mostly automatically for Dyck languages (Sec. 2.1, p.13896). For the human-annotated tasks, 300 traces per task were sampled with 85% wrong final answers (p.13896). Mistake finding: GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4, GPT-3.5-Turbo, Gemini Pro and PaLM 2 Unicorn, 3-shot, with three prompting methods (Sec. 3, pp. 13896-13897). Correction: PaLM 2 Unicorn backtracks on its own traces (Sec. 4, p.13899).
  - What it establishes:
    - The five models locate the first mistake poorly; a prediction counts only if the step matches exactly or the model correctly says there is none (Sec. 3, p.13896; Table 4, p.13897).
    - Backtracking: with the oracle mistake location, the model re-generates that step at temperature 1, retrying up to 8 times until the step differs, then continues greedily (Sec. 4, p.13899). This turns a sizeable share of wrong answers into right ones, with smaller losses among originally correct answers (backtracking is applied to any trace that contains a mistake, including correct-answer traces). Choosing the step at random also helps, but much less (Table 6, p.13900).
    - A small classifier (PaLM 2 Otter) fine-tuned on the other four tasks beats 3-shot PaLM 2 Unicorn at mistake finding on 4 of 5 held-out tasks (Table 7, p.13902), but stays below the 60-70% mistake-finding accuracy at which, in simulation, backtracking gains start to outweigh losses (Sec. 5.1, p.13901; Sec. 5.2, p.13901).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 4 "Overall" mistake-finding accuracy (direct trace-level / direct step-level / CoT step-level): GPT-4-Turbo 30.13 / 48.33 / not run; GPT-4 39.80 / 52.87 / 43.40; GPT-3.5-Turbo 10.44 / 14.78 / 14.31; Gemini Pro 16.14 / not run / not run; PaLM 2 Unicorn 17.09 / 23.67 / 24.65 (p.13897). Table 6, absolute change in answer accuracy with the oracle location, for originally correct and originally incorrect answers: word sorting -11.11 / +23.53; tracking shuffled objects -6.67 / +43.92; logical deduction -11.43 / +36.86; multistep arithmetic -0.00 / +18.04; Dyck languages -6.82 / +18.06. With a random location: -15.56 / +11.76, -6.67 / +20.39, -13.33 / +21.57, -8.89 / +10.59, -15.91 / +5.16 (p.13900).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: the tasks are artificial, chosen to keep mistakes unambiguous; backtracking was evaluated only on the skewed sample, not on the original BIG-Bench sets, and the work is framed as a proof of concept (Limitations, p.13903). Mistake finding by PaLM 2 on its own traces may be biased (footnote 6, p.13897). Backtracking is a deliberately simple baseline, and a fuller assessment is left to future work (pp. 13899-13900). Gains depend on the task's original accuracy: at 50% original accuracy on tracking shuffled objects the new accuracy would be 68.6%, at 99% it would fall to 92.8% (p.13900).
  - Relation to the review: supports RQ2 and the RQ3 discussion of recovery. A wrong answer can often be traced to one first mistake whose repair recovers the answer, which fits key decisions; and models cannot find that step themselves, which fits the review's point that recovery from a commitment is hard without outside help. The annotation ignores later errors because they may depend on the first one (Sec. 2.1, p.13895), which is the review's dependence assumption built into the protocol.
  - The hinge: repair is measured by re-sampling one step at an oracle location on five synthetic tasks with one generator. The paper does not measure how many steps per trace are decisive, and the random-location baseline shows that re-sampling other steps also recovers some answers.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Models struggled to identify the first logical mistake in chain-of-thought traces, but, given the location of the mistake, a backtracking method corrected many of the outputs."
  - Source passage: "GPT-4 attains the best results but only reaches an overall accuracy of 52.87" (Sec. 3.1, p.13897). Correction gains are in Table 6 (p.13900); the backtracking procedure is in Sec. 4 (p.13899).
  - Scope: mistake finding tested on five 2023-era models; correction tested only with PaLM 2 Unicorn on its own traces; five BIG-Bench tasks; dataset skewed toward wrong answers.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "On BIG-Bench Mistake, 2,186 chain-of-thought traces from PaLM 2 on five BIG-Bench tasks, each annotated with its first logical mistake, five LLMs located that mistake poorly (the best, GPT-4, reached 52.87% overall accuracy), yet when PaLM 2 was given the location and re-generated just that step before continuing, 18-44% of its originally wrong answers became correct, depending on the task, against 5-22% when the step was chosen at random, while 0-11% of its originally correct answers became wrong [tyen2024llms]." Shorter main-text variant: "LLMs located the first logical mistake in chain-of-thought traces poorly (at best 53% overall accuracy, for GPT-4), but when PaLM 2 was told where its mistake was and re-generated that step, 18-44% of its wrong answers became correct, depending on the task [tyen2024llms]."
- Bib notes: verified against the ACL Anthology .bib (https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.826.bib) and the Crossref record: five authors, title, pages 13894-13908, Bangkok, August 2024, DOI. The Anthology and the PDF byline spell the third author "Victor Carbune"; the arXiv metadata writes "Cărbune". The bib follows the Anthology; switch to C{\u{a}}rbune if the house style prefers the diacritic. Title kept in the Anthology's sentence case with {LLMs} protected.

### wang2024chain
- Source of record: https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/7a8e7fd295aa04eac4b470ae27f8785c-Abstract-Conference.html (NeurIPS 2024; Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 37, pp. 66383-66409; DOI 10.52202/079017-2123). Access: full text (NeurIPS PDF https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/7a8e7fd295aa04eac4b470ae27f8785c-Paper-Conference.pdf and arXiv v2 PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.10200v2; the cited numbers are identical).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref record 10.52202/079017-2123 has no update or retraction relation; arXiv shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: pre-trained PaLM-2 (X-Small to Large), Mistral-7B and Gemma-7B, plus instruction-tuned variants, prompted only with "Q: [question]\nA:" (Sec. 3, p.5). CoT-decoding takes the top k = 10 tokens at the first decoding step, continues each path greedily, and ranks paths by answer confidence: the probability gap between the top two tokens, averaged over the answer tokens (Sec. 2.2, pp. 3-4; Sec. 3, p.5). Tasks: GSM8K, MultiArith and year parity (Sec. 3, p.5), then synthetic tasks of graded difficulty: coin flip (from Wei et al.), web of lies and multi-step arithmetic (from Big-Bench-Hard), and sports understanding and object counting (from Big-Bench) (Sec. 3.2, pp. 7-8).
  - What it establishes:
    - Greedy paths mostly answer directly; among the alternative first tokens, continuing greedily often reveals a chain of thought (Sec. 2.1, p.3; Table 1).
    - Answer confidence is higher on paths with a chain of thought, and ranking by it extracts those paths better than ranking by log-probability or length-normalized log-probability (Sec. 2.2, Table 2, p.4).
    - Branching early matters. Later branching is constrained by the tokens already generated: in the paper's example, starting with the wrong answer token greatly lowers the chance of recovering, although mid-path branching works for year parity (Sec. 2.2 and Fig. 2, pp. 4-5).
    - Gains shrink with difficulty: correct chain-of-thought paths appear when the solution needs one or two steps of knowledge manipulation and become hard to find at three or more (Sec. 3.2, p.8).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): in a manual check of the first 100 GSM8K questions, 88% of the highest-confidence paths among the top 10 contain a chain of thought (Sec. 2.2, p.4; the sentence does not name the model, and the section's examples and Table 2 use pre-trained PaLM-2 Large). Table 2 (PaLM-2 L): GSM8K top-100, greedy 44.0% versus CoT-decoding 72.0%; year parity 57.0% versus 95.0% (p.4). Table 3 (GSM8K): Mistral-7B greedy 9.9%, self-consistency without a CoT prompt 12.9%, CoT-decoding 25.1%; PaLM-2 L 34.8%, 40.6%, 63.2% (p.4).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: exploring alternative paths costs extra computation; the top-two probability gap is less precise for open-ended answers; only the first token is branched; and for complex tasks or inadequately trained models none of the top-k tokens may lead to a useful path (Sec. 5, Discussion and Limitations, p.10).
  - Relation to the review: supports RQ2 and RQ3 with qualitative evidence. The first generated token works as a decision point (answer directly or reason first) that redirects the rest of the output, and the paper reports that later branching is constrained by what was already generated. The confidence signal sits on the answer tokens, not at the decision point.
  - The hinge: the setting is pre-trained models given a bare question-answer prompt, with branching only at the first token; the paper measures whether paths contain a chain of thought and how confident the final answer is, not where errors occur within a path.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Branching over the top alternatives at the first decoding step, then decoding greedily, often surfaced chain-of-thought paths, and the model's confidence in its final answer was higher on those paths."
  - Source passage: "the presence of a CoT path typically leads to a more confident decoding" (Sec. 2.2, p.3). The number: 88% of the highest-confidence paths contained a chain of thought in the manual check of 100 GSM8K questions (p.4).
  - Scope: pre-trained models with a plain QA prompt; k = 10 first-token alternatives; confidence measured as the top-two probability gap over the answer tokens; the 88% figure comes from 100 questions inspected by hand.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. Recommended wording with the number: "Branching over the top 10 alternatives for the first token and then decoding greedily often surfaced chain-of-thought paths in pre-trained models given a plain question-answer prompt, and the model was more confident in its final answer on those paths: in a manual check of 100 GSM8K questions, 88% of the highest-confidence paths contained a chain of thought [wang2024chain]."
- Bib notes: verified against the NeurIPS proceedings page and its BibTeX export (https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/23625-/bibtex) and the Crossref record: authors, title, volume 37, pages 66383-66409, DOI. The PDF header writes "without" in lower case; the bib follows the proceedings metadata ("Without").

### deepseek2025r1
- Source of record: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09422-z (Nature 645(8081), 633-638, 2025; published online 17 September 2025, issue of 18 September 2025; DOI 10.1038/s41586-025-09422-z; open access, CC BY 4.0). - Card:
  - Setting: DeepSeek-R1-Zero is trained from DeepSeek-V3-Base with GRPO and no supervised fine-tuning first; the reward is rule-based, an accuracy reward (a final answer in a set format checked against ground truth, or code checked against test cases) plus a format reward for the think tags (pp. 633-634; Methods, Reward design, PDF p.7). DeepSeek-R1 adds cold-start supervised data, rejection sampling and a second RL stage that also uses model-based rewards for general data (pp. 634-635; Methods, PDF p.8).
  - What it establishes:
    - Under these rewards R1-Zero's AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% during training, and to 86.7% with self-consistency (p.634; Fig. 1a).
    - Responses grew steadily longer during training (Fig. 1b, p.634), and the model increasingly showed reflective reasoning and systematic exploration of alternative solutions (p.634; Extended Data Fig. 1a).
    - The authors report an "aha moment" in an intermediate checkpoint, marked by a sudden rise in the use of "wait" during reflections (p.634; Table 1, p.635; Extended Data Fig. 1b, PDF p.9). In the Table 1 example the model stops mid-derivation and re-evaluates its earlier steps.
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): AIME 2024 pass@1 15.6% to 77.9%, 86.7% with self-consistency (p.634). Extended Data Fig. 1b: "wait" was virtually absent early in training, appeared sporadically between steps 4,000 and 7,000, and rose sharply after step 8,000; the caption reads these trends as suggesting self-monitoring behaviour (PDF p.9).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: R1-Zero has poor readability and mixes languages (pp. 633-634); R1 still overthinks simple questions (token efficiency) and is sensitive to prompts, with few-shot prompting degrading it (p.636); pure RL depends on reliable rewards and is open to reward hacking where rewards come from a model (p.637). The conclusion describes self-verification and reflection as seeming to emerge on their own during RL (p.636).
  - Relation to the review: qualifies RQ3. Within-answer dependence is the review's claim; R1 documents a training route by which models come to revisit their own earlier steps, consistent with the review's note that reasoning models partly escape self-conditioning. It is the standard reference for that class of models.
  - The hinge: the paper documents the appearance of reflective behaviour (an example, word frequencies, response length) and benchmark gains. It does not measure how often reflection finds and fixes an earlier error.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Reinforcement learning with rewards for verifiable answers produced reasoning traces in which the model re-examines and revises earlier steps."
  - Source passage: "incorporating verification, reflection and the exploration of alternative approaches within each response" (main text, p.633). The aha-moment description is on p.634 and the example in Table 1 (p.635). The reward is based only on the correctness of final predictions against ground-truth answers (p.633). The arXiv v1 (Sec. 2.2.4, p.8) described reflection as the model revisiting and re-evaluating its previous steps; that sentence is not in the Nature version.
  - Scope: DeepSeek-R1-Zero (RL only); rule-based accuracy and format rewards; evidence is one illustrated example plus word-frequency trends. Nothing in the Nature text says the model "revises" steps as a measured behaviour.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "Trained by reinforcement learning with rewards that checked only whether final answers were correct (plus output format), DeepSeek-R1-Zero came to produce longer reasoning traces that include verification, reflection and exploration of alternative approaches; the authors illustrate this with an "aha moment" in which the model stops mid-solution and re-evaluates its earlier steps, and describe such behaviours as appearing to emerge without being taught [deepseek2025r1]."

### leviathan2023fast
- Source of record: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/leviathan23a.html (ICML 2023, PMLR vol. 202, pp. 19274-19286; ICML oral per the arXiv comment). Access: full text (PMLR PDF https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/leviathan23a/leviathan23a.pdf and arXiv v2 PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.17192v2; the cited numbers are identical).
- Retraction: none found. PMLR assigns no DOI and Crossref has no record of the paper; the bibliographic and retraction-filtered queries return unrelated works; arXiv shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: target model T5-XXL (11B) on WMT English-German translation and CNN/DM summarization, with T5-small (77M), T5-base (250M) and T5-large (800M) as draft ("approximation") models; walltime measured at batch size 1 on one TPU-v4 against the T5X implementation, with argmax (temperature 0) and standard sampling (temperature 1) (Sec. 4.1, p.6). Acceptance rates were also measured for a 97M GPT-like model on lm1b and for LaMDA 137B on dialog (Sec. 4.2, p.7).
  - What it establishes:
    - The draft proposes gamma tokens; the target scores them in parallel; a rejection-sampling rule accepts a prefix and resamples the first rejected token from an adjusted distribution, so each target run yields at least one token (Sec. 2, p.2). Speculative sampling guarantees the target's output distribution for any draft model (Sec. 3.6, p.5; proof in App. A.1, p.11).
    - Acceptance rate (Definition 3.1, p.3): beta is the probability that a drafted token is accepted, and alpha is its expectation; beta = 1 - D_LK(p, q) = sum over x of min(p(x), q(x)) (Theorem 3.5 and Corollary 3.6, pp. 3-4). Assuming i.i.d. acceptances, a target run yields on average (1 - alpha^(gamma+1)) / (1 - alpha) tokens (Eq. 1, p.3), and the expected walltime gain is (1 - alpha^(gamma+1)) / ((1 - alpha)(gamma c + 1)), where c is the draft-to-target cost ratio (Theorem 3.8, p.4).
    - Drafts about two orders of magnitude smaller than the target give alpha between 0.5 and 0.9; sharper distributions give higher alpha; even a bigram draft reaches alpha 0.2 on translation (Sec. 4.2 and Table 3, p.7).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 2 (p.6), T5-XXL with a T5-small draft: translation 3.4X (temperature 0, gamma 7, alpha 0.75) and 2.6X (temperature 1, gamma 7, alpha 0.62); summarization 3.1X (temperature 0, gamma 5, alpha 0.65) and 2.3X (temperature 1, gamma 5, alpha 0.53). Across all drafts, speedups range from 1.4X to 3.4X. The abstract summarizes this as 2X-3X with identical outputs (p.1).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: latency improves at the cost of more arithmetic operations, so the method does not help without spare compute (Sec. 3.4, p.4; Sec. 6, p.8); the i.i.d. assumption is only an approximation (App. A.3, p.11); only drafts of the same architecture as the target were tested (Sec. 3.6, p.5).
  - Relation to the review: precedent for the design implication of RQ2. The method rests on the observation that some decoding steps are harder than others (Sec. 1, p.1), and the measured acceptance rates show that a much smaller model's distribution agrees with the large model's at most positions.
  - The hinge: alpha is an expected distributional overlap between draft and target, not a measure of which positions decide whether an answer is correct; outputs are unchanged by construction, so no error rate is measured.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "A smaller draft model proposes several tokens that the target model checks in parallel, which preserves the target model's output distribution exactly and gave 2–3× speedups on T5-XXL."
  - Source passage: "guarantee an identical output distribution for any choice of approximation model" (Sec. 3.6, p.5). The 2X-3X figure is the abstract's summary (p.1) of Table 2 (p.6).
  - Scope: T5-XXL on translation and summarization; batch size 1; one TPU-v4; speedup relative to the T5X implementation; requires spare compute.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. Recommended fuller wording: "In speculative decoding, a smaller draft model proposes several tokens that the target model verifies in parallel, and a rejection-sampling rule keeps the target model's output distribution exactly unchanged; on T5-XXL it cut walltime by a factor of 2 to 3 relative to the standard T5X implementation at batch size 1 [leviathan2023fast]."
- Bib notes: authors, title, booktitle (Proceedings of the 40th International Conference on Machine Learning), series, volume 202, pages 19274-19286, publisher PMLR and year verified against the BibTeX block on the PMLR paper page. Format follows the PMLR entries already in references.bib. No DOI exists.

### raposo2024mixture
- Source of record: https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02258 (arXiv preprint, single version v1, 2 April 2024). Venue: none found. OpenReview search returned no record of this paper at any venue, and a web-search claim of ICLR 2025 was false (the linked ICLR 2025 PDF is a different paper, "Mind over Body: Adaptive Thinking using Dynamic Computation"). Access: full text (arXiv v1 PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.02258v1).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref has no record of the title; the bibliographic and retraction-filtered queries return unrelated works; arXiv shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: decoder-only transformers from 60M to 3B parameters, trained at budgets of 6e18, 2e19 and 1e20 FLOPs with sequences of 2,048 tokens (Fig. 3 caption, p.8; Fig. 4, p.9). The training corpus is not named. A per-block router scores each token, and only the top-k tokens (a capacity fixed in advance) pass through that block's self-attention and MLP; the rest skip it on the residual stream (Sec. 3, pp. 4-7). Because top-k routing is non-causal, sampling uses an auxiliary loss or a small predictor of the router's decision (Sec. 3.5, p.7).
  - What it establishes:
    - The best configuration routes every other block with a capacity of 256 of 2,048 tokens (12.5%); routing every other block was crucial, and learned routing beat stochastic routing by a wide margin (Fig. 3 caption and Sec. 4.1, p.8).
    - A 220M MoD model slightly outperforms the isoFLOP-optimal 220M baseline while stepping more than 60% faster in training, in about the same wall-clock training time (Sec. 4.1, pp. 7-8). The intro reports up to 1.5% better final log probability at equal training FLOPs, or loss parity with a fraction (upwards of 50%) of the FLOPs per forward pass (Sec. 1, p.2).
    - With the causal predictor (above 97% accurate soon into training), autoregressive evaluation on 500M held-out tokens shows little degradation, and some MoD variants beat the isoFLOP-optimal baseline with fewer FLOPs per forward pass (Sec. 4.2, p.10; Fig. 6 caption, p.11).
    - The authors report a preliminary association: tokens that engage more blocks tend to have higher-entropy output predictions, which may correspond to harder predictions (Sec. 4.1, p.10).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): capacity 12.5% (256 of 2,048 tokens), every other block (Fig. 3 caption, p.8); model #3 equals the isoFLOP-optimal baseline but steps 66% faster (Fig. 3 caption, p.8); upwards of 50% faster to step during post-training sampling (Abstract, p.1).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: top-k routing is non-causal and needs a workaround for sampling, with the auxiliary loss costing about 0.2-0.3% of the language-modeling objective (Sec. 3.5, p.7); FLOPs cannot be dropped indiscriminately, since learned routing is required (Sec. 5, p.11); the routing-entropy link is described as preliminary (p.10).
  - Relation to the review: precedent for the system-design section and suggestive for RQ1/RQ2. Full computation for an eighth of the tokens in alternate blocks costs nothing in language-modeling loss, and the routing analysis tentatively links extra computation to higher-entropy predictions.
  - The hinge: the 12.5% is a capacity set in advance and tuned, not a measured share of key positions; the outcome is language-modeling loss, not the correctness of a generated answer.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Routing only a capped fraction of tokens through each block matched the baseline at fewer floating-point operations per forward pass."
  - Source passage: "match baseline performance for equivalent FLOPS and wall-clock times to train" (Abstract, p.1). The same sentence adds that these models need a fraction of the FLOPs per forward pass. The routing is applied in every other block, interleaved with full-capacity blocks (Fig. 3 caption, p.8; Fig. 5 caption, p.10).
  - Scope: routing in every other block, not each block; performance measured as training loss and held-out log probability at matched training FLOPs; models up to 3B parameters.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "In Mixture-of-Depths transformers, a learned router lets only a fixed fraction of tokens (12.5% in the best configuration) through the attention and MLP of every other block while the rest skip it; at equal training compute these models matched or slightly beat the language-modeling loss of the best standard transformer while using fewer FLOPs per forward pass [raposo2024mixture]."
- Bib notes: six authors, their order and spelling ("Peter Conway Humphreys"), the title and the year verified on the arXiv abs page and the PDF title block. arXiv-only; entered as @misc with the arXiv DOI.

### laban2025lost
- Source of record: https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/59f6421e64707225fdf5b28840679a07-Abstract-Conference.html (ICLR 2026, oral per the OpenReview record https://openreview.net/forum?id=VKGTGGcwl6, venue "ICLR 2026 Oral"; proceedings pp. 54738-54778; no DOI). arXiv 2505.06120 has one version (v1, 9 May 2025). Access: full text (ICLR camera-ready PDF https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/file/59f6421e64707225fdf5b28840679a07-Paper-Conference.pdf and arXiv v1 PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.06120v1; the cited numbers agree). The OpenReview PDF was behind a challenge page and was not used.
- Retraction: none found. Crossref has no record of the paper; the top bibliographic match is a different paper that builds on it ("MT-OSC", Findings of ACL 2026); the retraction-filtered results are unrelated; arXiv shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: six generation tasks (code, database queries, API actions, math, data-to-text, summarization), about 100 instructions each, 15 LLMs from eight families including o3 and DeepSeek-R1, ten simulations per instruction at temperature 1, over 200,000 conversations (Sec. 4.3, p.6). Each fully specified instruction is split into "shards"; in the SHARDED setting an LLM user simulator (GPT-4o-mini) reveals at most one shard per turn, and the conversation's score is the maximum over all answer attempts (Sec. 3.2, pp. 3-4). FULL gives the whole instruction at once; CONCAT gives all shards at once as a bullet list (Sec. 3.3, p.4). Metrics per instruction over the ten runs: mean performance P, aptitude A (90th percentile score) and unreliability U (90th minus 10th percentile) (Sec. 4.2, p.6).
  - What it establishes:
    - Every model loses performance on every task from FULL to SHARDED, by 39% on average; CONCAT keeps 95.1% of FULL performance, so the loss is not caused by information lost in sharding (Sec. 5.1, pp. 7-8; Table 1).
    - The drop is mostly increased unreliability: aptitude falls by 16% on average while unreliability more than doubles (+112%), and all models show similarly high unreliability in multi-turn settings (Sec. 5.2, p.8).
    - Candidate causes (App. F, pp. 22-24): premature full answer attempts that make assumptions, over-reliance on earlier incorrect attempts with answers growing "bloated", over-weighting of the first and last turns, and overly verbose answers. On Code and Math, conversations in which the first answer attempt comes in the first 20% of turns score 30.9, against 64.4 when it comes in the last 20%; the authors hypothesize that incorrect assumptions in premature solutions conflict with later instructions (App. F.1, Table 3, pp. 22-23).
    - The effect appears from two shards onward (gradual sharding, Sec. 5.3, p.9), and lowering the temperature to 0 does not remove it: multi-turn unreliability remains high (about 30 points), which the authors attribute to cascading effects of early variation (Sec. 6, p.9).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): average degradation -39% (Sec. 5.1, p.7); aptitude -16% and unreliability +112% (Sec. 5.2, p.8; Fig. 1 labels the aptitude drop -15%, p.1); about 50 points between best and worst run for a fixed instruction in multi-turn (p.8); first-attempt timing 30.9 versus 64.4 (Table 3, p.22). The introduction also reports average performance of 65% multi-turn against 90% single-turn, a 25-point drop (Sec. 1, p.2), which is a different aggregate from the 39% relative figure.
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: the findings come from a controlled underspecification stress test with simulated users, not from natural dialogue; sharded conversations follow a narrow structure, reveal one shard per turn and always end fully specified; minimal shards may be unrealistic (Ethics Statement, p.10).
  - Relation to the review: supports RQ3 at the level of a conversation. An early, assumption-laden answer attempt tends to be carried forward rather than revised, and even at temperature 0 early variation cascades. The setting adds information turn by turn, which is close to the review's reading of agentic decay as a function of decisions rather than tokens.
  - The hinge: degradation is measured per conversation as a relative score change between settings with an LLM-simulated user, not as errors at token or step positions; the causal account rests on correlational analyses (the premature-attempt analysis covers only Code and Math) plus the authors' hypotheses.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "When the information for a task was revealed over several turns instead of all at once, performance dropped by about 39% on average, largely because models made early assumptions and did not recover from them."
  - Source passage: "LLMs often make assumptions in early turns and prematurely attempt to generate final solutions" (Abstract, p.1). The abstract continues that models overly rely on these attempts, and summarizes the finding as models getting lost and not recovering after a wrong turn. The paper's decomposition attributes the drop in large part to increased unreliability rather than lost aptitude (Sec. 5.2, p.8); early assumptions are one of four candidate causes (App. F, p.22).
  - Scope: 15 LLMs; six generation tasks; LLM-simulated users; relative degradation averaged over tasks and models; best-of-turns scoring.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "When a simulated user revealed a task's requirements over several turns instead of all at once, 15 LLMs scored 39% lower on average across six generation tasks; the authors attribute the drop mainly to greater variability between runs rather than lower best-case ability, and find that models often make assumptions and attempt full solutions early, then rely too heavily on those attempts instead of recovering [laban2025lost]."
- Bib notes: verified against the ICLR proceedings page and its BibTeX export (https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/8039-/bibtex) and the OpenReview record (venue "ICLR 2026 Oral", venueid ICLR.cc/2026/Conference): four authors, title, pages 54738-54778, year 2026. The key says 2025 (the arXiv year); the entry's year is 2026. Booktitle follows the house style ("The Fourteenth International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR)"). A Microsoft Research social-media post and a blog report a best-paper award; I did not confirm this on an ICLR page and the card does not rely on it.

### lanham2023measuring
- Source of record: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702 (arXiv preprint, single version v1, 17 July 2023, by authors at Anthropic and one at Oxford; no peer-reviewed version found, and the OpenReview search lists only the DBLP CoRR record). Access: full text (arXiv v1 PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.13702v1).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref has no record of the title (the top bibliographic matches are other faithfulness papers); the retraction-filtered results are unrelated; arXiv shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: a 175B-parameter pretrained decoder-only model fine-tuned with RLHF as a dialog assistant; mistakes are written by the pretrained model without RLHF (Sec. 2.1, p.2). Eight multiple-choice tasks: AQuA, LogiQA, MMLU, HellaSwag, TruthfulQA, OpenBookQA, ARC Challenge and ARC Easy (pp. 2-3). For each question, 100 chain-of-thought samples (nucleus p = 0.95, temperature 0.8), split into sentences; the average chain has 4 steps, and 89% have three to six (Sec. 2.1-2.2, p.3). Early answering truncates the chain after each sentence and asks for the answer; adding mistakes replaces one sentence with an LLM-written mistaken version, re-samples the rest of the chain, and asks for the answer (Sec. 2.3-2.4, pp. 3-4). Section 3 repeats a with-versus-without-CoT measure across RLHF models of several sizes (pp. 7-8).
  - What it establishes:
    - How much the answer depends on the chain of thought varies widely by task: in the early-answering test the chain changes the final answer less than 10% of the time for ARC Easy, ARC Challenge and OpenBookQA, but more than 60% of the time for AQuA (Sec. 2.3.1, p.4).
    - Adding mistakes gives nearly the same task ordering, and the dependence does not track the accuracy gain from CoT (Sec. 2.4.1, p.5; Table 2).
    - Filler tokens bring no accuracy gain, and paraphrasing the chain leaves accuracy nearly unchanged (Sec. 2.5-2.6, pp. 6-7).
    - For 7 of 8 tasks, the 13B model changes its answer after CoT more often than the 175B model, so dependence on the chain falls with model size on most tasks (Sec. 3.1, p.7).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 2 (p.5), area-over-curve for early answering / adding mistakes: AQuA 0.44 / 0.52; LogiQA 0.26 / 0.31; MMLU 0.12 / 0.21; HellaSwag 0.12 / 0.23; TruthfulQA 0.11 / 0.20; OpenBookQA 0.07 / 0.15; ARC (Challenge) 0.05 / 0.11; ARC (Easy) 0.02 / 0.07. Accuracy difference from CoT ranges from -4.69 (HellaSwag) to 15.32 (AQuA) points.
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: there is no independent access to the model's internal reasoning, so the tests rule out classes of unfaithfulness rather than prove faithfulness, and the hypotheses may not be exhaustive; the models studied are RLHF-tuned, and pretrained models might condition more strongly on their own text, for example changing their answer more often when mistakes are added (Sec. 5, p.9). The mistake generator produces a plausible mistake at least 80% of the time (Sec. 2.4, p.4).
  - Relation to the review: qualifies RQ3. Whether an error inserted into the reasoning reaches the final answer depends strongly on the task and on model size; on some tasks the answer barely depends on the stated chain, so an error in the chain need not propagate.
  - The hinge: the paper measures faithfulness under experimenter interventions (truncation, inserted mistakes followed by re-sampling) on multiple-choice tasks with short chains, for one 175B model in Section 2; it does not observe naturally occurring errors.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Truncating a chain of thought, or inserting a mistake into it, changed the final answer to an extent that varied widely across tasks."
  - Source passage: "the extent of post-hoc reasoning varies considerably between tasks" (Sec. 2.4.1, p.5), stated for the adding-mistakes test and said to match the early-answering result. Numbers in Sec. 2.3.1 (p.4) and Table 2 (p.5).
  - Scope: one 175B RLHF model for the truncation and mistake tests; eight multiple-choice tasks; chains of about four sentences.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. Recommended fuller wording: "For a 175B-parameter RLHF model on eight multiple-choice tasks, truncating its chain of thought or inserting a mistake into it changed the final answer to very different extents across tasks: in the truncation test, the chain changed the answer less than 10% of the time on ARC and OpenBookQA but more than 60% of the time on AQuA [lanham2023measuring]."
- Bib notes: all 30 authors, their order and spelling, the title and the year verified on the arXiv abs page and the PDF title block. The PDF prints "Kamile Lukosiute" without diacritics; the arXiv metadata gives "Kamilė Lukošiūtė", which the bib encodes in LaTeX. arXiv-only; entered as @misc with the arXiv DOI.
