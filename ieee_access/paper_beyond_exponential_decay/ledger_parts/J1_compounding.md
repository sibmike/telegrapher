### ross2010efficient
- Source of record: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/ross10a.html (AISTATS 2010; PMLR vol. 9, pp. 661-668, which appeared at the time as JMLR W&CP 9). Access: full text (https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/ross10a/ross10a.pdf; supplementary material https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/ross10a/ross10aSupple.pdf).
- Retraction: none found. The paper has no Crossref DOI. The bibliographic query's top 3 results are unrelated works, and the retraction-filtered query matches nothing with this title. There is no arXiv version.
- Card:
  - Setting: theory of imitation learning by reduction to classification. Tasks last T steps and the expert π* is deterministic. The per-step cost C(s,a) lies in [0,1], and J(π) is the expected total cost over T steps. ε is the expected 0-1 disagreement with the expert, measured on the expert's own state distribution d_π* (Sec. 2, p. 662). The experiments are Super Tux Kart steering from images with a human expert, and Mario Bros. with a near-optimal planner as expert; both use single-hidden-layer neural-network classifiers (Sec. 5, pp. 666-668).
  - What it establishes:
    - Theorem 2.1 (Sec. 2.1, p. 662), restated: if the learned policy π̂ has expected 0-1 loss at most ε under d_π*, then J(π̂) ≤ J(π*) + T²ε. The proof (supplement) charges the maximal cost of 1 at every step after π̂'s first mistake.
    - Tightness (Sec. 2.1, p. 662): the authors state that the bound is tight. They describe an instance with expected T-step cost (1 − εT)J(π*) + T²ε and also point to Kääriäinen (2006). The 3-state example in the supplement (ε ≤ 1/T) incurs a cost of exactly T²ε.
    - Two interactive remedies. Forward Training learns a non-stationary policy and gives J(π^T) ≤ J(π*) + Tε_u, where u_i bounds how much changing the action at step i can raise the cost-to-go. In the worst case u_i = T − i + 1 and the guarantee is no better than the quadratic one; when the expert recovers within k steps (u_i ≤ k), the regret is O(kTε̄) (Sec. 3, pp. 663-664). SMILe trains a stochastic stationary mixture and reaches O(T(Ã₁ + ε̃) + 1) with N = 2T²(ln T)^{3/2} iterations (Theorem 4.1, p. 665).
  - Headline numbers (with location): the bound T²ε (Theorem 2.1, p. 662). The tight instance's cost is (1 − εT)J(π*) + T²ε (p. 662); the supplement's example costs T²ε. In Super Tux Kart, the supervised approach needs 45 laps of data before it performs reasonably, while SMILe with α = 0.1 reaches similar performance after 5 iterations (15 laps) (Sec. 5, p. 667).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: the theorems are worst-case upper bounds. The authors note that even in favourable, recoverable cases the traditional approach can still have quadratic regret in T (Sec. 3, pp. 663-664; example in the supplement). Forward Training is impractical when T is large (p. 664). Throughout, they assume the learner can query the expert at any state (Sec. 1, p. 662). Their analysis requires only that the "state" be a sufficient statistic of the history (footnote 6, p. 668).
  - Relation to the review: a precedent. This is the formal origin, in imitation learning, of the compounding-error argument that the review examines. The quadratic term comes from the worst case: one error takes the learner off the expert's distribution, and it then pays the maximal cost at every later step without recovering. The following is our reading, not the authors': in the tight 3-state example, the learner's entire error rate sits in one early state that the expert visits with frequency 1/T. One mistake there decides the episode. That is closer to one key decision followed by lock-in than to uniform per-step risk.
  - The hinge: the bound counts the expected total cost summed over T steps against a deterministic expert, with ε measured on the expert's own state distribution. The review's quantity is the probability that a whole answer is correct.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "In imitation learning, a policy trained only on the states an expert visits can incur a cost that grows quadratically with the task horizon T, because one mistake can take it to states the expert never visited."
  - Source passage: "it could end up in new states that were not visited by π*" (Sec. 2.1, p. 662; the authors' intuition for Theorem 2.1). The same page gives J(π̂) ≤ J(π*) + T²ε and states that the bound is tight. The abstract describes the effect as compounding errors with regret quadratic in the horizon (p. 661).
  - Scope: this is a worst case over tasks. ε is the per-step error rate on the expert's own states. The quadratic term is the excess cost over the expert, for fixed ε, as T grows. The bound holds in general. Its tight instances are ones where the learner never recovers after its first mistake, and the proof charges cost 1 at every later step.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. Recommended wording, which keeps the worst-case reading and names ε: "In imitation learning, a policy trained only on the states an expert visits, and wrong on a fraction ε of them, can incur an excess cost over the expert as large as T²ε, quadratic in the task horizon T, because one mistake can take it to states the expert never visited; Ross and Bagnell show that this bound is tight [ross2010efficient]."
- Bib notes: title, pages 661-668, volume 9, and year verified on the PMLR page and its BibTeX export. PMLR lists the authors as Stephane Ross and Drew Bagnell; the entry uses the PDF byline, Stéphane Ross and J. Andrew Bagnell. The booktitle follows PMLR (Thirteenth AISTATS, Chia Laguna Resort, Sardinia, Italy, 13-15 May 2010). No DOI exists.

### ross2011dagger
- Source of record: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v15/ross11a.html (AISTATS 2011, PMLR vol. 15, pp. 627-635). Access: full text (https://proceedings.mlr.press/v15/ross11a/ross11a.pdf). arXiv:1011.0686 (v3, March 2011, whose comment gives AISTATS 2011): metadata only.
- Retraction: none found. The paper has no Crossref DOI; the bibliographic and retraction-filtered queries match nothing with this title. arXiv shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: the paper reduces imitation learning and structured prediction to no-regret online learning. Tasks last T steps, and the task cost C lies in [0,1]. The learner minimizes a surrogate loss ℓ (0-1, hinge, or squared) against the expert's actions, and an interactive expert labels any state the learner visits (Sec. 2, p. 628). DAgger runs the current policy, optionally mixed with the expert at rate β_i, has the expert label the visited states, aggregates all data, and retrains (Algorithm 3.1, p. 630). The experiments are Super Tux Kart (linear controller), Super Mario Bros. (linear SVMs), and handwriting recognition treated as structured prediction (Sec. 5, pp. 632-634).
  - What it establishes:
    - Theorem 3.2 (Sec. 3, p. 630), restated: if N is Õ(uT), some policy among the iterates has J(π̂) ≤ J(π*) + uTε_N + O(1). Here ε_N is the true surrogate loss of the best policy in hindsight on the state distributions the iterates induced. u bounds how much taking any single other action at a reachable state raises the expert's cost-to-go (Theorem 2.2, p. 629). When the task cost is the imitation loss, or is bounded by it, Theorem 3.1 gives J(π̂) ≤ Tε_N + O(1) directly (p. 630).
    - Assumptions (Sec. 3, p. 630; Sec. 4.2, p. 631): ℓ is strongly convex and bounded over the policy class, so that Follow-the-Leader is no-regret, and for Theorem 3.2 ℓ also upper-bounds the 0-1 loss. The mixing weights satisfy β_i ≤ (1 − α)^{i−1} with α independent of T, and each iteration has infinitely many samples. Theorems 3.3-3.4 are finite-sample versions needing O(T² log(1/δ)) and O(u²T² log(1/δ)) iterations. The authors call the need for a no-regret method or a strongly convex loss a stronger, though common, assumption than plain classification reductions (p. 631).
    - On u (Sec. 2.2, p. 629; stated for the forward algorithm, whose bound has the same uTε form): in the worst case u can be O(T), and there is then no gain over supervised training. u is O(1) when the expert can recover from the learner's mistakes within a few steps, for example in rapidly mixing MDPs (footnote 2, p. 629).
  - Headline numbers (with location): the excess cost uTε_N + O(1) (Theorem 3.2, p. 630). Restating Ross and Bagnell, a classifier with error ε on the expert's states can make as many as T²ε mistakes over T steps (Sec. 1, p. 627). Footnote 1 (p. 628) gives Kääriäinen's sequence-prediction example: a predictor trained with the previous correct output as input, at error rate ε, makes T/2 − (1 − (1 − 2ε)^{T+1})/(4ε) + 1/2 expected mistakes at test time, which is Θ(T²ε) for small ε. In handwriting recognition, character accuracy is 82% without structure, 83.6% for the supervised baseline trained on correct previous characters, and 85.5% for DAgger (Sec. 5.3, p. 634).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: the guarantee needs the no-regret or strongly-convex assumption (p. 631) and an expert who can be queried on the learner's states. The linear rate needs u to be O(1) (p. 629). The bound holds for the best policy in the sequence, or for one drawn uniformly at random (footnote 3, p. 630), not necessarily for the last.
  - Relation to the review: a precedent. What turns T²ε into uTε is u, the most that one wrong action can cost when the expert would otherwise recover. Our reading, not the authors': u is a per-decision cost of error, close to the review's split between key and non-key decisions, and a u of order T corresponds to a decision whose error cannot be undone. Footnote 1 is the teacher-forcing, or exposure-bias, form of the quadratic bound.
  - The hinge: the result needs an interactive expert that labels the learner's own states, a no-regret learner on a strongly convex surrogate, and a bounded u. The review's quantity is whether the whole sequence is correct, not the expected total cost.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Training on the states the learner itself visits, with the expert labeling them (DAgger), gives a bound that grows linearly in the horizon, under the paper's assumptions."
  - Source passage: "number of mistakes/costs that grows linearly in T and classification cost ϵ" (Sec. 1, p. 628). Formally, Theorem 3.2 (p. 630) bounds the excess cost by uTε_N + O(1), which is linear in T only when u is O(1) (Sec. 2.2, p. 629).
  - Scope: the bound requires an interactive expert, a strongly convex and bounded surrogate loss with a no-regret learner, decaying β_i, and Õ(uT) iterations. It is linear in T when u = O(1), as in recoverable tasks, or when the task cost is the imitation loss itself. With u = O(T) it is quadratic.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "Training on the states the learner itself visits, with an interactive expert labeling them (DAgger), bounds the excess cost by uTε_N, where ε_N is the lowest surrogate loss any policy in the class attains on those states and u is the most that a single wrong action can raise the expert's cost-to-go; the bound is linear in the horizon T when u stays bounded, as when the expert can recover from the learner's mistakes within a few steps [ross2011dagger]." Why narrowed: "under the paper's assumptions" hides the condition that carries the result. Theorem 3.2 is stated in terms of u, and the paper notes that u can be of order T.
- Bib notes: title, author list, pages 627-635, volume 15, and year verified on the PMLR page and its BibTeX export. PMLR lists Stephane Ross, Geoffrey Gordon, and Drew Bagnell; the entry uses the PDF byline, Stéphane Ross, Geoffrey J. Gordon, and J. Andrew Bagnell. No DOI exists.

### foster2024behavior
- Source of record: https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/da84e39ae51fd26bb5110d9659c06e13-Abstract-Conference.html (NeurIPS 2024; Advances in NeurIPS 37, pp. 120602-120666; DOI 10.52202/079017-3834). Venue CONFIRMED by the proceedings page, the NeurIPS BibTeX export, and the Crossref record; the arXiv comment also gives NeurIPS 2024. Access: full text (the camera-ready PDF https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/da84e39ae51fd26bb5110d9659c06e13-Paper-Conference.pdf, 65 pages with appendices). I also checked the arXiv abs page for 2407.15007 (v2 of 30 Nov 2024).
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref record 10.52202/079017-3834 has no update-to, updated-by, or relation entries. arXiv shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: a learning-theoretic analysis of offline imitation learning in episodic MDPs with horizon H. The policy classes Π are general (finite, for presentation), the setting is realizable (the expert is in Π), and rewards are normalized so that the cumulative reward lies in [0, R]; dense rewards give R = H (Sec. 1.1, pp. 2-4). Behavior cloning with the log loss (LogLossBC) is maximum likelihood over trajectories (Sec. 2.1, p. 6). App. B.3 treats language-model next-token prediction as LogLossBC (p. 20). The experiments are MuJoCo Walker2d, Atari BeamRider, a Car environment, and Dyck-language generation with small GPT-2-style transformers (App. C, pp. 20-24).
  - What it establishes:
    - Deterministic experts: J(π*) − J(π̂) ≤ 4R·D²_H(P_π̂, P_π*), where D²_H is the squared Hellinger distance between trajectory distributions (Theorem 2.1, p. 6). LogLossBC then gives J(π*) − J(π̂) ≤ 8R·log(2|Π|δ⁻¹)/n with n expert trajectories (Corollary 2.1, p. 7). With parameter sharing (log|Π| = O(1)), this is horizon-independent under sparse rewards and linear in H under dense rewards. Without parameter sharing (log|Π| ∝ H), it is quadratic in H under dense rewards, which is optimal for offline IL (Sec. 2.3, p. 7; Table 1, p. 5).
    - A lower bound of order H/n holds even for online algorithms with recoverability µ = 1 (Theorem 2.2, p. 7). Online access (Dagger) therefore does not improve on LogLossBC for general classes with parameter sharing (Sec. 2.4, pp. 7-8).
    - Stochastic experts: the regret is controlled by the expert's variance σ² (Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.1, p. 9). Under dense rewards, both offline and online learners need on the order of H² trajectories in the worst case (Theorem 3.2, p. 9). If a signed recoverability constant µ̃ is O(1), O(H log|Π|/ε²) trajectories suffice (p. 9).
  - Headline numbers (with location): Theorem 2.1's bound 4R·D²_H (p. 6) and Corollary 2.1's 8R·log(2|Π|δ⁻¹)/n (p. 7). Table 1 (p. 5) gives O(R log|Π|/n) and O(H log|Π|/n) with parameter sharing, and O(HR log max_h|Π_h|/n) and O(H² log max_h|Π_h|/n) without it. In the experiments, regret is largely independent of H for Walker2d (H = 50-500) and BeamRider (H = 50-300) (Fig. 1, p. 2; App. C.2, p. 23). For Dyck, with n fixed, regret increases with H; the authors attribute this to the supervised-learning error itself growing with H (App. C.2, p. 23).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: they focus on the realizable setting and leave a thorough study of misspecification out of scope (Sec. 1.1.1, p. 3). They note that their results may not account for practical factors such as misspecification or geometric structure (Sec. 2.4, p. 8). Whether D²_H is a suitable proxy for real-world performance, including for language models, is left open (Sec. 4, p. 10). For autoregressive language models, they write only that they expect the error amplification seen in practice to come from difficulty in minimizing D²_H (architecture, data, optimization) rather than from fundamental limits of next-token prediction (App. B.3, p. 20). This is a hedged expectation, not a result.
  - Relation to the review: this qualifies the compounding argument at the level of theory. Under the paper's assumptions, log-loss training does not by itself force quadratic or exponential dependence on length; the horizon enters through the trajectory-level divergence D²_H. For a task scored only on whether the final answer is right (R = 1), App. B.3 notes that rollout performance has no explicit dependence on sequence length except through D²_H (p. 20). Whether D²_H grows with length in practice remains open, and the Dyck experiment shows that it can.
  - The hinge: the results assume realizability, a policy class whose complexity does not grow with H (parameter sharing), a normalized cumulative reward, and, for the linear dense-reward rate, a deterministic expert. Error here means sample complexity (how many expert trajectories a given regret needs), not a fixed per-step error rate.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "With the logarithmic loss, and under conditions the authors state, the error of behavior cloning need not grow quadratically with the horizon."
  - Source passage: "possible to achieve horizon-independent sample complexity in offline IL" (Abstract, p. 1). The same sentence names the conditions: the range of cumulative payoffs is controlled, and a supervised-learning complexity of the policy class is controlled. The rates are in Theorem 2.1, Corollary 2.1 (pp. 6-7), and Table 1 (p. 5).
  - Scope: the setting is realizable. The linear dense-reward rate needs a deterministic expert, and horizon independence needs parameter sharing. For stochastic experts under dense rewards, quadratic dependence is necessary in the worst case unless µ̃ = O(1). "Error" here means regret relative to the expert, as a function of the number of expert trajectories.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "Foster et al. show that behavior cloning with the logarithmic loss, the objective of next-token prediction, need not suffer quadratic horizon dependence: when the expert lies in the policy class, the total reward is bounded, and the class's complexity does not grow with the horizon (as with parameter sharing), its sample complexity is independent of the horizon, and for a deterministic expert with per-step rewards it is linear. For stochastic experts with per-step rewards, they show that quadratic dependence is unavoidable in the worst case unless a recoverability-like condition holds [foster2024behavior]." Why narrowed: "under conditions the authors state" has to name them. The stochastic-expert exception matters for language models, whose training distribution is stochastic. Do not cite this paper as showing that LLM errors do not compound; in its own Dyck experiment, regret rises with length.
- Bib notes: verified against the NeurIPS proceedings BibTeX export (authors, title, volume 37, pages 120602-120666, DOI) and the Crossref record. The abstract on the proceedings web page calls the offline/online gap not fundamental, while the PDF abstract calls it smaller than previously thought; quote the PDF. The capital U after the question mark is braced so that IEEEtran's sentence casing keeps it.

### ranzato2016sequence
- Source of record: ICLR 2016 conference track (poster). The paper is listed on the accepted-papers page https://iclr.cc/archive/www/doku.php%3Fid=iclr2016:accepted-main.html. The text of record is arXiv:1511.06732 v7 (6 May 2016), whose PDF header states that it was published as an ICLR 2016 conference paper. Venue CONFIRMED. ICLR 2016 has no formal proceedings volume, pages, or DOI. Access: full text (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.06732, v7; abs page https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06732).
- Retraction: none found. There is no Crossref record; the arXiv DOI 10.48550/arXiv.1511.06732 is registered with DataCite. The bibliographic query returns only unrelated works. arXiv shows seven versions and no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: conditional RNN text generators with attentive encoders: an Elman RNN with 128 hidden units for summarization, and LSTMs with 256 and 512 units for translation and captioning. The tasks are Gigaword headline generation (targets of about 10 words, unrolled to 15), IWSLT 2014 German-English translation (unrolled to 25), and MSCOCO captioning (unrolled to 15) (Sec. 4, pp. 8-9). The proposed method, MIXER, starts from cross-entropy (XENT) training and anneals toward REINFORCE on the model's own samples, rewarded with BLEU or ROUGE-2 at the sequence level (Sec. 3.2.2, pp. 7-8; Algorithm 1, p. 8).
  - What it establishes:
    - The paper names the train-generate discrepancy exposure bias: the model is trained on ground-truth previous words but generates from its own. It asserts that errors along the way will quickly accumulate (Sec. 1, p. 1). It cites earlier work on the problem: Daumé III et al. 2009 (SEARN), Ross et al. 2011, and Bengio et al. 2015 (scheduled sampling) together with DAD (Sec. 2, p. 2).
    - MIXER both trains on the model's own predictions and optimizes the test metric. Under greedy decoding it beats XENT, DAD, and an end-to-end backpropagation baseline on all three tasks, and it is competitive with beam search (Sec. 4.4, pp. 9-10).
  - Headline numbers (with location; greedy decoding; Fig. 5 table, p. 10): summarization ROUGE-2 13.01 (XENT) vs 16.22 (MIXER); translation BLEU-4 17.74 vs 20.73; image captioning BLEU-4 27.8 vs 29.16.
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: REINFORCE started from a random policy never converged, so the mixed loss with incremental learning was necessary (Sec. 4.4, p. 10). Better estimates of the average reward and more thorough search at training time are left for future work (Sec. 5, p. 11). The paper does not measure error accumulation directly.
  - Relation to the review: a precedent for the term and for the compounding claim in neural text generation. The accumulation is asserted in the introduction, not measured. The evaluation uses sequence-level BLEU and ROUGE, and MIXER changes both the training inputs and the loss, so its gains do not isolate exposure bias. The authors themselves conclude that the experiments show the value of optimizing the metric used at test time (Sec. 4.4, p. 9).
  - The hinge: small RNN generators produce short outputs (about 10-30 tokens) under greedy decoding. The evidence is a metric gain from a combined change to training, not a measurement of how errors accumulate with length.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Ranzato et al. named the mismatch between training on ground-truth prefixes and generating from the model's own outputs exposure bias."
  - Source passage: "We refer to this discrepancy as exposure bias" (Sec. 1, p. 1). The discrepancy is that training inputs come from the data distribution, while at test time the model consumes its own generated words (same paragraph).
  - Scope: this concerns the name only. The discrepancy itself was described earlier, in work the paper cites (Sec. 2, p. 2). As a side check, the full text of Bengio et al. 2015 (arXiv:1506.03099) describes the discrepancy but never uses the term exposure bias. That paper is not audited as a ledger entry.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. Keep "named"; do not write "identified" or "discovered".
- Bib notes: authors and title verified on the arXiv abs page and PDF. ICLR 2016 conference-track acceptance comes from the iclr.cc accepted-papers list, where the paper appears among the poster presentations. The location and dates (San Juan, Puerto Rico, 2-4 May 2016) come from the ICLR 2016 main page on iclr.cc. The entry has no pages or DOI, and its url points to arXiv. DBLP was not consulted.

### schmidt2019generalization
- Source of record: https://aclanthology.org/D19-5616/ (Proceedings of the 3rd Workshop on Neural Generation and Translation, WNGT 2019, Hong Kong; pp. 157-167; DOI 10.18653/v1/D19-5616). Venue CONFIRMED. Access: full text (https://aclanthology.org/D19-5616.pdf).
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref record 10.18653/v1/d19-5616 has no update or relation entries.
- Card:
  - Setting: a paper that argues a position and proposes a method. It first argues about teacher forcing and exposure bias (Sec. 3, pp. 159-160). It then proposes latent ERPO, an entropy-regularized RL formulation (after Tan et al.) combined with a sentence VAE and a word2vec-based reward; it trains on contexts that mix ground truth with model predictions (Sec. 4, pp. 160-162). The experiments cover unconditional language modeling and sentence auto-encoding with a 256-dimensional GRU on a 1M-sentence BooksCorpus subset (90-10 split, 40K vocabulary), evaluated by sequence NLL (Sec. 6, pp. 163-164).
  - What it establishes:
    - Teacher forcing follows directly from maximum-likelihood training with the chain rule. As long as log-likelihood is the only criterion, there is no case for training on generated contexts (Sec. 1, p. 157; Sec. 3, p. 160).
    - Proposition 1 (Sec. 3, p. 160): exposure bias describes a lack of generalization with respect to some measure other than likelihood, usually implicit and possibly task-dependent. Remedies that replace, mask, or perturb ground-truth contexts act as regularizers toward that measure (Sec. 1, p. 157; Sec. 3, p. 160).
    - Exposure bias had not been separated from ordinary test log-likelihood assessment. Citing He et al. 2019 (the arXiv precursor of he2021exposure), the paper reports that studies of explicit effects had found nothing significant (Sec. 1, p. 157).
  - Headline numbers (with location; Table 1, p. 164; test NLL): the proposed latent model 52.54, RAML 52.56, scheduled sampling (p = 0.99) 52.60, VAE 52.66, deterministic language model 53.01. The author attributes the whole gain over RAML to using model-predicted contexts during training (Sec. 6.2, p. 164).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: training is very sensitive to the α and β settings and easily collapses to plain maximum likelihood (Sec. 6, p. 163). The framework does not on its own overcome VAE collapse (Sec. 6.2, p. 164). A human evaluation is left for future work (p. 165). The focus is unconditional generation; for conditional tasks such as translation, regularizing the mapping is usually enough (Sec. 1, p. 157; Sec. 2.1, p. 158).
  - Relation to the review: this qualifies the exposure-bias framing. It treats the train-test discrepancy as a question of generalization rather than an intrinsic defect of teacher forcing, and it notes that direct evidence for exposure bias was lacking at the time.
  - The hinge: the paper offers a conceptual argument with small unconditional experiments scored by likelihood. It does not measure error accumulation or answer correctness.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Schmidt argued that exposure bias is better understood as a failure to generalize than as a train–test mismatch to be fixed by training on model samples."
  - Source passage: "Exposure bias describes a lack of generalization" (Proposition 1, Sec. 3, p. 160). The conclusion (p. 164) says exposure bias does not point to a problem with the standard training method; it refers to a kind of generalization to unseen sequences that log-likelihood does not capture.
  - Scope: the first half of the sentence is Schmidt's thesis. The second half overstates it. He does not argue against training on model samples: he reads such remedies as implicit regularization, his own method trains on model-predicted contexts, and he credits those contexts for its gain over RAML (Sec. 6.2, p. 164).
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "Schmidt argued that teacher forcing is simply what maximum-likelihood training of an autoregressive model entails, and that exposure bias is better understood as a lack of generalization, with respect to some usually implicit measure other than likelihood, than as a defect of that training; he read the proposed remedies, which replace or perturb ground-truth contexts, as forms of regularization [schmidt2019generalization]."
- Bib notes: the ACL Anthology .bib (title, single author, booktitle, pages 157-167, DOI, address Hong Kong, publisher ACL) and the Crossref record agree. The title keeps the original capitalization; under IEEEtran's sentence casing it renders correctly without braces.

### he2021exposure
- Source of record: https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.415/ (EMNLP 2021, Online and Punta Cana; pp. 5087-5102; DOI 10.18653/v1/2021.emnlp-main.415). Venue CONFIRMED. Access: full text (https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.415.pdf). I also checked the arXiv abs page for 1905.10617 (v10, 3 Sep 2021).
- Retraction: none for the published paper; the Crossref record has no update or relation entries. - Card:
  - Setting: open-ended continuation of WikiText-103 text, with a 20-token prompt, a 20-100-token prefix, and a 20-token generation. The models are a 16-layer Transformer-XL trained on half of WikiText-103 (test perplexity 27.81) and a 3-layer LSTM (perplexity 34.80). For the EB-C metric, a synthetic setup has a 4-layer Transformer-XL (perplexity 84) learn from samples of a 16-layer Transformer-XL (perplexity 24) that plays the data distribution (Sec. 6.1, p. 5091). Decoding is ancestral sampling, with top-k (k = 40) as a check (Sec. 3, p. 5089; Sec. 6.2, p. 5092).
    - EB-M is the ratio of continuation quality or diversity (BLEU, Nist, METEOR, backward-BLEU, n-gram entropy) given data prefixes versus model prefixes.
    - EB-C is the corresponding ratio of next-token divergence from the data distribution (TV, JS) (Sec. 5, pp. 5090-5091).
    - Crowdworkers also rated quality and consistency on Mechanical Turk (Sec. 6.3, p. 5093).
  - What it establishes:
    - Replacing the model's own prefix with a ground-truth prefix improves the continuation only slightly, and the gap does not grow with prefix length up to 100 tokens (Sec. 6.2, p. 5092; Table 2).
    - Larger artificial discrepancies do hurt: shuffled or randomly corrupted data prefixes markedly degrade the continuation (Sec. 6.2, p. 5092; Fig. 2, p. 5093).
    - Self-recovery: when the model continues past a shuffled or corrupted prefix, the degradation measures show a clear decreasing trend as the gap between the prefix and the scored span grows to 60 tokens (Sec. 6.4, Fig. 3, p. 5094). The authors hypothesize that this self-recovery counters exposure bias (Hypothesis 1, p. 5094).
  - Headline numbers (with location):
    - EB-M is at or below about 1.01 for both models, and EB-C at or below about 1.02, a relative gain of about 1-2% (Sec. 6.2, p. 5092; Table 2 caption).
    - With 30% of prefix tokens corrupted, EB-M shows a relative loss of about 10% (Sec. 6.2, p. 5092).
    - In the human ratings, the quality gap is about 1% and the consistency gap about 3%, with no strong increasing trend over prefix lengths of 20, 40, and 60 (Sec. 6.3 and Table 3, p. 5093).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: the metrics are only proxies, because exposure bias was never rigorously defined, and a unigram LM would pass the test (Sec. 8, p. 5095). The study covers open-ended generation only, and exposure bias may be more serious in other kinds of generation task (Sec. 8, p. 5095). Comparisons against reference prefixes can mislead in constrained tasks such as dialogue and translation (Sec. 7, pp. 5094-5095).

Within a sequence, a distorted prefix degrades what follows, but the effect fades with distance rather than compounding. That holds at least for open-ended text from small 2019-era LMs under sampling. Xu et al. (xu2022loop, Sec. 5, p. 9) note that He et al. used ancestral sampling, while under maximization-based decoding a repeated sentence reinforces itself. The two results differ in decoding.

Recovery is measured as a falling degradation ratio over gaps of up to 60 tokens; the paper reports a trend, not the extent of recovery. ### xu2022loop
- Source of record: https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/148c0aeea1c5da82f4fa86a09d4190da-Abstract-Conference.html (NeurIPS 2022; Advances in NeurIPS 35, pp. 3082-3095; DOI 10.52202/068431-0223). Venue CONFIRMED by the proceedings page, the BibTeX export, and Crossref; the arXiv comment also gives acceptance at NeurIPS 2022. Access: full text (the camera-ready PDF https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/148c0aeea1c5da82f4fa86a09d4190da-Paper-Conference.pdf and the supplement https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/148c0aeea1c5da82f4fa86a09d4190da-Supplemental-Conference.pdf).
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref record has no update or relation entries, and arXiv 2206.02369 (v2) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: the analysis model is a 16-layer, 750M-parameter word-level Transformer LM trained on WikiText-103 with MLE, following Welleck et al. (Sec. 2.1, p. 4; Sec. 4.1, p. 6).
    - Probe: a sentence from one of three corpora (random tokens, BookCorpus, and the WikiText-103 dev set; 1,000 sentences each) is repeated up to 100 times in the context. The authors record the probabilities of the tokens in the next repetition (Sec. 2.1, pp. 3-4).
    - Metrics: TP is the average token probability. IP is the share of tokens whose probability rose above their first occurrence. WR, the winner rate, is the share that both rose and are the argmax (p. 4).
    - The method, DITTO, fine-tunes the model with a loss that penalizes repetition probability on pseudo-repetitive data (Sec. 3, p. 5). It is evaluated on WikiText-103 continuation and on CNN/DailyMail summarization with BART-large (Sec. 4, pp. 6-8).
  - What it establishes:
    - A single earlier occurrence already raises the probability of repeating a sentence in most cases: IP at the first repetition exceeds 90% for all three corpora (Sec. 2.2, p. 4; Fig. 3b).
    - Self-reinforcement: TP, IP, and WR rise monotonically with the number of repetitions in the context and level off at ceiling values (Sec. 2.2, p. 5; Fig. 3, p. 4). Sentences with higher initial probability reinforce faster (p. 5; App. D.1). One loop that the model generated itself under greedy decoding shows the same pattern (supplement, App. B).
    - The authors' explanation: maximization-based decoding picks high-probability sentences, which are likely to be repeated, and each repetition makes the next more likely, so the model gets stuck. Stochastic sampling partly avoids this because sampled sentences have lower likelihood (Sec. 2.2, p. 5).
  - Headline numbers (with location): consecutive sentence-level repetition in human text is 0.02% in WikiText-103 (Abstract, p. 1). With greedy decoding, the MLE baseline repeats 14.50% of sentences, against 2.85% after DITTO and 0.01% in human text; its MAUVE score is 0.34, against 0.77 after DITTO (Table 1, p. 7).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: the analysis relates token probability to the repetition count but does not explain why the model raises that probability; embeddings, architecture, and properties of language are left for future work (Sec. 6, p. 10). The phenomenon is tied to maximization-based decoding (Sec. 1, p. 1; Sec. 2.2, p. 5).
  - Relation to the review: this supports RQ3, dependence within an answer. It measures a positive-feedback mechanism: once a repetition error occurs, the same error becomes more likely at the next opportunity, so errors within a sequence are not independent. In Sec. 5 (p. 9) the authors contrast their result with he2021exposure's self-recovery and attribute the difference to decoding (ancestral sampling versus maximization-based decoding).
  - The hinge: one 750M-parameter model trained on WikiText-103, probed with artificially repeated sentences. The errors are degenerate repetitions under greedy decoding, not factual or reasoning errors.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "The probability that a model repeats a sentence rises with the number of times it has already repeated it, a self-reinforcing effect."
  - Source passage: "the probability of repetition increases almost monotonically with the number of historical repetitions" (Sec. 1, p. 2). Sec. 2.2 (p. 5) names this the self-reinforcement effect, and Fig. 3 (p. 4) shows the curves levelling off at a ceiling.
  - Scope: one model (750M parameters, trained on WikiText-103). The repetitions were mostly inserted by hand, plus examples the model generated under greedy decoding. The increase is almost monotonic and saturates.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "In a 750M-parameter Transformer language model trained on WikiText-103, Xu et al. found that the more often a sentence already appears in the context, the more probable its next repetition becomes, rising almost monotonically to a ceiling; they call this a self-reinforcement effect and identify it as what traps greedy decoding in sentence-level loops [xu2022loop]."
- Bib notes: the NeurIPS BibTeX export and Crossref agree on the authors, title, volume 35, pages 3082-3095, and DOI. The export's title has a trailing space, which the entry drops.

### bachmann2024pitfalls (additional use)
- Source of record: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/bachmann24a.html (ICML 2024; PMLR 235, pp. 2296-2318; re-confirmed on the PMLR page today). Access: full text (PMLR PDF https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlresearch/v235/main/assets/bachmann24a/bachmann24a.pdf). I also checked the arXiv abs page for 2403.06963 (v3 of 29 Jul 2025; comment ICML 2024).
- Retraction: none found. The PMLR paper has no Crossref DOI, the bibliographic query returns only unrelated works, and arXiv shows no withdrawal. This matches ledger part E.
- Card (framing only; the paper's setting and Use 1 are in ledger part E):
  - Setting: as in part E. The task is a synthetic path-star graph; GPT-Mini and Mamba are trained from scratch, and GPT-2 Large is fine-tuned.
  - What it establishes (for this use):
    - The authors call autoregressive inference and teacher-forced training two phases of next-token prediction that are often conflated and must be treated separately (Abstract, p. 1; Contribution 2, p. 2). They say the compounding-error argument does not carefully distinguish the two (Sec. 1, p. 1).
    - They attribute the compounding critique to Kääriäinen (2006), Ross and Bagnell (2010), LeCun (2024), and Dziri et al. (2024) (Sec. 1, p. 1; Sec. 3, p. 3; Sec. 6, p. 9). LeCun is cited as a 2024 university lecture, *Do large language models need sensory grounding for meaning and understanding?*, not as the LinkedIn post that the review cites under lecun2023why. Their Failure 1 formalizes snowballing as P(r̂ = r) ≈ (1 − ε)^L (Sec. 3, p. 3), which is the review's target model.
    - In their view, compounding is only a shallow failure to execute a plan at inference, which a verify-and-backtrack wrapper might fix. The deeper problem is that, in lookahead tasks, teacher forcing can fail to learn an accurate next-token predictor at all (Sec. 1, pp. 1-2; Sec. 3, p. 3). The two failures are orthogonal: each can occur without the other (App. B, Proposition 4, p. 15). The authors class scheduled sampling, DAgger, and SEARN-style training on model-generated sequences as remedies for the snowball failure only (App. H, p. 22).
  - Headline numbers (with location): the introduction's example puts the chance of at least one error over 200 tokens at 0.86 when the per-token error is 0.01 (Sec. 1, p. 1). On the path-star task the error occurs at the first step, with accuracy near 1/d for a start node of degree d (App. B, p. 15; Sec. 6, p. 9).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope: the failure is shown on a minimal synthetic task, and the authors say it is unclear whether it extends to ordinary text generation (App. A, p. 15). See part E.
  - Relation to the review: this supports the review's distinction between failure at one key decision and accumulation over many positions. In their task the error sits at the first token, and the rest of the path follows from it. It is also a precedent for separating training-time and inference-time accounts of failure.
  - The hinge: a lookahead task built so that teacher forcing permits a shortcut (the Clever Hans cheat). The authors' judgment about which problem runs deeper is an argument backed by this minimal task, not a measurement on general text or reasoning.
- Use 2 — intended claim: "Bachmann and Nagarajan separate two failures that the compounding argument runs together: errors that snowball during generation, and a failure of teacher-forced training to learn the task, which they argue is the more basic problem."
  - Source passage: "This assumption sidesteps a more deep-rooted problem we expose" (Abstract, PMLR PDF p. 1). The assumption is that teacher forcing has learned an accurate next-token predictor, which, the same paragraph says, the compounding criticism presupposes. The conflation is described in the Abstract and Sec. 1 (pp. 1-2); the orthogonality in Sec. 4.5 (p. 6), Sec. 6 (p. 9), and App. B (p. 15).
  - Scope: the teacher-forcing failure is claimed for certain classes of tasks, namely those that require lookahead, and is shown on a minimal path-finding task. The authors call the two failures orthogonal, with different remedies, and do not dispute that snowballing occurs.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "Bachmann and Nagarajan argue that the compounding-error critique, which they trace to Kääriäinen, Ross and Bagnell, LeCun, and Dziri et al., runs together two phases of next-token prediction that fail in different ways: errors can snowball during autoregressive generation, but in tasks that require planning ahead, teacher-forced training can fail to learn an accurate next-token predictor at all, a problem they consider more deep-rooted [bachmann2024pitfalls]." Why narrowed: the teacher-forcing failure is claimed for lookahead tasks, not for tasks in general. The authors' word is "more deep-rooted", and they treat the two failures as orthogonal rather than one underlying the other.
