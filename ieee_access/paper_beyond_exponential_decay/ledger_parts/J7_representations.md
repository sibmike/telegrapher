### raghu2017svcca
- Source of record: https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/hash/dc6a7e655d7e5840e66733e9ee67cc69-Abstract.html (NIPS 2017, Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 30, pp. 6076-6085 per the proceedings Metadata.json). Access: full text (https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/dc6a7e655d7e5840e66733e9ee67cc69-Paper.pdf; appendices from arXiv v2, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.05806).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref bibliographic queries (title; title plus authors) return no record for this paper (NeurIPS 2017 papers carry no DOI) and no notices. The arXiv abs page (v2, 8 Nov 2017) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: A neuron is represented by its activation vector over a finite set of inputs, and a layer by the subspace those vectors span. SVCCA first keeps each layer's top singular directions (enough to explain 99% of variance) and then runs CCA on the two reduced subspaces (Sec. 2, p. 3). Experiments: a conv net and a ResNet on CIFAR-10, a toy 1-D regression MLP, and an ImageNet ResNet; an LSTM on Penn Treebank appears in the appendix.
  - What it establishes:
    - It gives a similarity method for any two sets of neurons, applied across training time, random initializations, architectures and classes (Sec. 2, p. 3; Sec. 4.4, Fig. 7, conv net vs. ResNet, p. 7).
    - Networks converge from the bottom up: lower layers settle into their final representations first, which motivates "Freeze Training" (Sec. 4.1-4.2, Figs. 4-5).
    - A few SVCCA directions carry most of a layer's task-relevant information (Sec. 2.1, Fig. 2a, p. 4).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): For a 512-neuron fully connected layer of a CIFAR-10 conv net, the first 25 SVCCA directions give nearly the same accuracy as all 512 (Sec. 2.1, p. 4). The SVD step keeps directions explaining 99% of variance (Sec. 2, p. 3).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): The body ascribes affine invariance to the CCA step specifically (Sec. 2.1, p. 4), and Appendix B proves orthonormal and scaling invariance of CCA (arXiv v2, p. 11). The DFT speed-up assumes translation-invariant data and circular boundary conditions (Sec. 3, Theorem 1). Across random initializations, the first and last layers are similar while middle layers differ (App. E, Fig. App.4, arXiv v2, p. 16).
  - Relation to the review: A precedent for RQ3's untested geometry candidate. It offers a way to ask whether two networks, or two training runs, share representational structure. It measures similarity of representations, not co-occurrence of errors.
  - The hinge: It compares layer activations on a shared set of inputs in small vision networks and a toy regression network. The review's question is whether LLM errors co-occur across positions, samples and models.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "SVCCA compares the representations of layers within and across networks in a way that is invariant to affine transformations."
  - Source passage: "CCA is invariant to affine transformations, enabling comparisons without natural alignment" (Sec. 2.1, p. 4). The abstract describes the whole SVCCA tool as invariant to affine transform (p. 1). Kornblith et al. (2019, Sec. 4, SVCCA paragraph, PMLR p. 5) point out that, because SVCCA keeps a fixed fraction of the variance, it is invariant to invertible linear transformation only when the retained subspace does not change.
  - Scope: The within-network and across-network part is fully supported (Sec. 2, p. 3; Sec. 4.1; Sec. 4.4, p. 7). The invariance is exact for the CCA step, not for the SVD truncation in front of it.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "SVCCA compares the representations of layers within and across networks (over training, across random initializations and across architectures). It first keeps each layer's top singular directions and then applies canonical correlation analysis, whose invariance to affine transformations allows layers with no natural alignment to be compared [raghu2017svcca]." If the strict property matters, add: "Because of the truncation step, the full method is invariant to invertible linear maps only when the retained subspace is unchanged [kornblith2019similarity]."
- Bib notes: Authors, title and volume 30 from the proceedings BibTeX (NIPS2017_dc6a7e65). That BibTeX leaves pages empty, so pages 6076-6085 come from the proceedings Metadata.json (page_first/page_last). No DOI. The venue was still called NIPS in 2017; the series title is Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 30.

### kornblith2019similarity
- Source of record: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/kornblith19a.html (ICML 2019, PMLR 97:3519-3529). Access: full text (https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/kornblith19a/kornblith19a.pdf, Table 2 read from the rendered p. 6; Appendix F from arXiv v4, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.00414).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref queries return no record (PMLR assigns no DOIs) and no notices. The arXiv abs page (v4, 19 Jul 2019) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: The paper compares similarity indexes for neural representations: CCA (two summary statistics), SVCCA, PWCCA, linear regression, linear HSIC, and CKA (linear and RBF kernels). Its sanity check uses 10 architecturally identical 10-layer CNNs (All-CNN-C style) trained on CIFAR-10 from different random initializations, with logits excluded. Appendix F.1 repeats the check on 12-sublayer Transformer encoders trained on WMT18 English-German. Further studies cover depth, width, ResNets, and CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100.
  - What it establishes:
    - No statistic that is invariant to invertible linear transformation, CCA included, can measure meaningful similarity when the representation has more dimensions than there are data points (Abstract, p. 1; Sec. 2.1).
    - In the CNN sanity check, CKA identifies the corresponding layer across initializations and the CCA-based indexes do not (Sec. 6.1, Table 2, Fig. 2, p. 6).
    - In Transformer encoders all indexes pass the same check. CCA misses scale differences between attention and feed-forward sublayers because it is invariant to non-isotropic scaling (Sec. 6.1, p. 6; App. F.1, arXiv p. 18).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 2 (PMLR p. 6), accuracy (%) of identifying the corresponding layer by maximum similarity: CCA (mean rho) 1.4; CCA (R2_CCA) 10.6; SVCCA (mean rho) 9.9; SVCCA (R2_CCA) 15.1; PWCCA 11.1; Linear Reg. 45.4; Linear HSIC 22.2; CKA (Linear) 99.3; CKA (RBF 0.2) 80.6; CKA (RBF 0.4) 99.1; CKA (RBF 0.8) 99.3. The SVCCA truncation threshold was 0.99 (Table 2 caption).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): No SVCCA truncation threshold revealed the layer structure, consistent with Appendix E of Raghu et al. (Sec. 6.1; App. F.2). In Transformers every index passed, and RBF CKA and R2_CCA did slightly better than the rest (Sec. 6.1; App. F.1).
  - Relation to the review: A precedent for RQ3's geometry candidate. Networks trained from different seeds develop corresponding layer representations, and CKA can detect them. Corresponding representations are not the same thing as correlated errors.
  - The hinge: It measures similarity of layer activations on shared inputs in image CNNs that differ only in their seed. The review asks whether wrong outputs coincide across LLM samples and models.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Centered kernel alignment (CKA) reliably identifies corresponding layers in networks trained from different initializations, where measures based on canonical correlation do not."
  - Source passage: "CKA passes our sanity check, but other methods perform substantially worse" (Sec. 6.1, p. 6). The abstract makes the general statement. Table 2 gives the numbers above. Appendix F.1 reports that all indexes, including the CCA-based ones, pass the check for Transformer encoders.
  - Scope: The claim holds for the 10-layer CIFAR-10 CNNs (Table 2). It does not hold for Transformer encoders, where CCA-based indexes also succeed. RBF CKA at sigma = 0.2 of the median distance reached only 80.6%.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "In ten architecturally identical 10-layer CNNs trained on CIFAR-10 from different random initializations, centered kernel alignment matched each layer to its architectural counterpart about 99% of the time (linear CKA, 99.3%). Indexes based on canonical correlation (CCA, SVCCA, PWCCA) did so 1.4-15.1% of the time. In Transformer encoders, all the indexes passed this check [kornblith2019similarity]."
- Bib notes: From the PMLR BibTeX (pmlr-v97-kornblith19a): authors (PMLR lists "Hinton, Geoffrey" with no middle initial), title, Proceedings of the 36th ICML, PMLR vol. 97, pp. 3519-3529, 2019. No DOI.

### lenc2015understanding
- Source of record: https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2015/html/Lenc_Understanding_Image_Representations_2015_CVPR_paper.html (CVPR 2015). The IEEE record (DOI 10.1109/CVPR.2015.7298701, via Crossref) gives 2015 IEEE Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition, pp. 991-999. Access: full text (CVF PDF, https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2015/papers/Lenc_Understanding_Image_Representations_2015_CVPR_paper.pdf; Table 4 read from the rendered p. 7, printed page 997).
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref record for the DOI has no update relations, and the `updates:` filter returns 0. The arXiv abs page (1411.5908 v2) shows no withdrawal. An extended journal version exists: International Journal of Computer Vision, 2018, pp. 456-476, DOI 10.1007/s11263-018-1098-y (Crossref; not read here; it also has no update relations).
- Card:
  - Setting: HOG features and AlexNet-style CNNs. ALEXN (MatConvNet) is the reference network. The others are IMNET (also trained on ILSVRC12), PLCS (trained on MIT Places) and PLCS-H (Places plus ILSVRC12). The paper studies equivariance, invariance and equivalence, measured on the ILSVRC12 validation set.
  - What it establishes:
    - It defines stitching layers: a learned map that lets the top of one network run on the bottom of another. In a CNN the map can be a bank of linear filters (Sec. 2.3, p. 4, printed p. 994).
    - Conv1 and Conv2 are interchangeable between all tested pairs, while Conv5 is not fully interchangeable, especially from the Places network (Sec. 3.3, Table 4, p. 7).
    - Without a learned map (identity), the stitched network's top-1 error exceeds 99% (Sec. 3.3, p. 7).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 4 (p. 7), top-1/top-5 error. The unmodified ALEXN scores 0.43/0.20 (Table 2 caption). IMNET→ALEXN: Conv1 0.43/0.20, Conv2 0.46/0.22, Conv3 0.46/0.22, Conv4 0.46/0.22, Conv5 0.50/0.25. PLCS→ALEXN: 0.43/0.20, 0.47/0.23, 0.50/0.25, 0.54/0.29, 0.65/0.39. PLCS-H→ALEXN: 0.43/0.20, 0.46/0.22, 0.47/0.23, 0.49/0.24, 0.52/0.27.
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): The compared networks have similar but not identical structure and entirely different parameters (Sec. 3.3). Deeper layers are more task-specific, so they are less interchangeable (Sec. 3.3; Sec. 4).
  - Relation to the review: A precedent for RQ3's geometry candidate. Separately trained networks, even ones trained on different data, compute early representations that one learned linear map makes interchangeable.
  - The hinge: The setting is image classifiers from one architecture family, and stitching is scored by classification error. The review concerns token-level decisions in LLM generation.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Lenc and Vedaldi introduced stitching: the early layers of one convolutional network can be swapped into another through a learned linear map, with little loss."
  - Source passage: "Conv1 and Conv2 are interchangeable in all cases, whereas Conv5 is not fully interchangeable" (Sec. 3.3, p. 7). The abstract lists stitching layers among the methods the paper introduces, and Bansal et al. (2021, Sec. 1) credit Lenc and Vedaldi with introducing stitching. The stitching layer is a bank of linear filters learned from ILSVRC12 training images (Sec. 2.3; Sec. 3.3).
  - Scope: AlexNet-style networks trained on ImageNet and Places. "Little loss" holds for the early layers: with Conv1 or Conv2 swapped, top-1 error is 0.43-0.47, against 0.43 for the original network.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. Recommended addition for precision: "(AlexNet-style ImageNet and Places networks; deeper layers were less interchangeable)".
- Bib notes: Authors, title (CVF title case), booktitle and year from the CVF BibTeX, which has no pages. Pages 991-999, the DOI and the IEEE booktitle come from Crossref. Cite the CVPR paper; the IJCV 2018 extension is a separate publication.

### bansal2021revisiting
- Source of record: https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/hash/01ded4259d101feb739b06c399e9cd9c-Abstract.html (NeurIPS 2021, Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 34, pp. 225-236). Access: full text (https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2021/file/01ded4259d101feb739b06c399e9cd9c-Paper.pdf; arXiv v1, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.07682, also downloaded).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref queries return no record (no DOI) and no notices. The arXiv abs page (v1) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: The stitching layer is a 1x1 convolution with BatchNorm before and after it, inserted between ResNet blocks; for transformers it is a token-wise linear map. The stitching layer is trained with Adam on the task's training set. CIFAR-10 models: ResNet-18, 0.5x and 2x width variants, ResNet-164, Myrtle-CNN, and a ViT pretrained on CIFAR-5m. ImageNet models: ResNet-50 trained end-to-end (supervised) and self-supervised models (SimCLR, SwAV, DINO and others) with linear heads.
  - What it establishes:
    - Networks of one architecture trained with independent seeds (and independent training samples) can be stitched at every layer with test error close to that of the base models, a property the authors call "stitching connectivity" (Sec. 4, Fig. 2A, p. 7).
    - Supervised and self-supervised ResNet-50s on ImageNet are stitching-connected at all layers. With coarse labels or label noise, only the first half of the layers stitch as well as those of a standard model (Sec. 5, Figs. 2B and 3A, p. 8).
    - Bottom layers trained with more samples, greater width or more epochs can be plugged into a weaker model and improve it. For width this works only from wider to thinner (Sec. 6, Figs. 2C, 3B-C, pp. 8-9).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 1 (p. 5) compares stitching with CKA. Different initialization: stitching penalty up to 3% error, while CKA varies and can be 0. Self-supervision: stitching penalty up to 5% error, while CKA ranges 0.35-0.9. More data, time or width: stitching gives better performance, while CKA is far (can be 0 for data, 0.7 for width). Sec. 5: for SimCLR ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10, the maximum stitching penalty is 3% while CKA varies from 0.35 to 0.9 across layers. Sec. 6: models trained on 5K, 10K and 25K CIFAR-10 samples were stitched into the 10K model, and width multipliers were 0.25x, 1x and 2x (CKA 0.7-0.9 between the 0.25x and 2x models).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): Stitching requires training, so it costs more than CKA. Stitching across different architectures is tricky and needs a careful choice of stitching family. All results are in the vision domain (Sec. 7, p. 9). The stitching family is kept low-capacity, and a randomly initialized bottom network serves as a control and shows a high penalty (Sec. 2).
  - Relation to the review: A precedent for RQ3's geometry candidate. Differently trained networks converge on compatible internal representations (the authors' "Anna Karenina" scenario), which is one route by which models could come to share errors. The paper does not measure errors.
  - The hinge: Vision models with a shared architecture, stitched at block boundaries. The review's dependence question concerns co-occurring errors in LLM outputs.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Networks trained in different ways, for example with different seeds or objectives, can be stitched with little loss, and representations learned with more data or larger models stitch well into weaker ones."
  - Source passage: "good networks of the same architecture, but trained in very different ways" (Abstract, p. 1). Each part of the claim is supported separately. Seeds: Sec. 4, Fig. 2A. Objectives: supervised vs. self-supervised, Sec. 5, Fig. 2B. Label changes affect later layers: Sec. 5, Fig. 3A. More data: Sec. 6, Fig. 2C. Width: Sec. 6, Fig. 3C, wider into thinner only. Training time: Sec. 6, Fig. 3B.
  - Scope: All networks within a comparison share one architecture, and "larger" means wider versions of ResNet-18. All experiments are in vision (CIFAR-10, ImageNet). The stitcher is a 1x1 convolution. "Little loss" means a penalty of up to about 3% (seeds) or 5% (self-supervised vs. supervised). "Stitch well into weaker ones" means the stitched model beats the weaker model.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "In vision networks of a shared architecture, Bansal et al. found that models trained from different random seeds, or with supervised versus self-supervised objectives, could be joined at every layer through a learned 1x1-convolution stitching layer at small cost (at most about 3% and 5% error, respectively). Bottom layers trained with more data, greater width or longer training, plugged into a weaker model of the same family, improved it [bansal2021revisiting]."
- Bib notes: From the NeurIPS proceedings BibTeX (NEURIPS2021_01ded425): authors, title, vol. 34, pp. 225-236, Curran Associates. Crossref has no DOI for it.

### conneau2018word
- Source of record: https://openreview.net/forum?id=H196sainb (ICLR 2018; the DBLP record mirrored on OpenReview lists it as "ICLR (Poster) 2018"; OpenReview BibTeX booktitle "International Conference on Learning Representations", 2018). Access: full text (arXiv v3, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.04087, 30 Jan 2018, which carries the ICLR 2018 conference header; Table 1 read from the rendered p. 6). The OpenReview PDF returned HTTP 403 and was not opened.
- Retraction: none found. Crossref queries return no record (ICLR assigns no DOIs) and no notices. The arXiv abs page (v3) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: 300-dimensional fastText embeddings trained on Wikipedia in each language, restricted to the 200k most frequent words. Language pairs are English with Spanish, French, German, Russian, Chinese, Esperanto, and Italian in Table 2. Tasks are word translation (P@1 over 1,500 source queries against 200k targets), sentence translation retrieval, and cross-lingual word similarity.
  - What it establishes:
    - The method has three steps (Sec. 1, p. 2; Sec. 2.1-2.2; Sec. 3.3-3.5). A linear map W is learned adversarially against a discriminator and kept close to orthogonal by an extra update rule. W is then refined with the closed-form orthogonal Procrustes solution on a synthetic dictionary of mutual nearest neighbours under the CSLS similarity. Finally, an unsupervised criterion selects models.
    - Without supervision, the method matches or beats supervised Procrustes on several language pairs (Table 1, p. 6; Sec. 1).
    - Embeddings trained on the same corpus with different seeds can be aligned perfectly. Alignment is harder across different corpora, such as Wikipedia vs. Gigaword (App., Fig. 3, p. 14).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 1 (p. 6), P@1, unsupervised Adv-Refine-CSLS vs. supervised Procrustes-CSLS: en-es 81.7 vs. 81.4; es-en 83.3 vs. 82.9; en-fr 82.3 vs. 81.1; fr-en 82.1 vs. 82.4; en-de 74.0 vs. 73.5; de-en 72.2 vs. 72.4; en-ru 44.0 vs. 51.7; ru-en 59.1 vs. 63.7; en-zh 32.5 vs. 42.7; zh-en 31.4 vs. 36.7; en-eo 28.2 vs. 29.3; eo-en 25.6 vs. 25.3. Sec. 1 (p. 2): English-Italian 66.2% vs. 63.7% for the best supervised approach (200k vocabularies).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): Embeddings trained on Wikipedia in both languages gave a large boost, which the authors attribute to similar co-occurrence statistics (Sec. 4, p. 8; App., Fig. 3). The evaluation dictionaries ignore polysemy, which lowers the English-Esperanto scores (Sec. 4, p. 9). Distant pairs (en-ru, en-zh) remain below the supervised baseline (Table 1).
  - Relation to the review: A precedent for RQ3's geometry candidate. Embedding spaces trained independently are close to isometric and can be aligned by a rotation. The appendix finding that same-corpus spaces trained with different seeds align perfectly is the closest analogue here to representations shared across models.
  - The hinge: The objects are static word-embedding spaces aligned by one global orthogonal map. The review concerns contextual LLM states and whether errors co-occur.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "The word-embedding spaces of different languages were aligned by an orthogonal map learned without any parallel data."
  - Source passage: "ensure that the matrix W stays close to an orthogonal matrix during training" (Sec. 3.3, p. 5). The method uses only two monolingual corpora and no cross-lingual annotated data (Sec. 1, p. 2). Adversarial training comes first and Procrustes refinement second (Sec. 1, p. 2; Sec. 2.1-2.2). The Fig. 1 caption describes the adversarial step as learning a rotation that the Procrustes step then refines (p. 3).
  - Scope: fastText Wikipedia embeddings. The adversarial map is kept near-orthogonal, and the refined map is the orthogonal Procrustes solution. Model selection is unsupervised. Bilingual dictionaries are used only for evaluation.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. Recommended wording, which names the method: "Conneau et al. aligned monolingual word-embedding spaces of different languages without parallel data. An adversarially learned linear map, kept close to orthogonal, was refined with the closed-form orthogonal Procrustes solution on a synthetic dictionary [conneau2018word]."
- Bib notes: The author order varies by source. The paper byline (arXiv v3) and Meta AI's publication page list Conneau, Lample, Ranzato, Denoyer, Jégou, and a footnote says the first two contributed equally with the order set by a coin flip. OpenReview's metadata and DBLP's ICLR 2018 record list Lample first. The BibTeX uses the byline order, which matches the assigned key. Venue: ICLR 2018, OpenReview H196sainb, with no pages or DOI.

### azaria2023internal
- Source of record: https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.68/ (Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2023, Singapore, pp. 967-976, DOI 10.18653/v1/2023.findings-emnlp.68). Access: full text (https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.68.pdf; Tables 1-4 read from the rendered pp. 6-7, printed pp. 972-973). arXiv v2 (2304.13734) was downloaded but not needed.
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref record for the DOI has no relations, and the `updates:` filter returns 0. The arXiv abs page (v2, Oct 2023) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: The True-False dataset has 6,084 statements in six topics (cities, inventions, chemical elements, animals, companies, scientific facts), mostly built from templates. SAPLMA is a feed-forward classifier (256-128-64 units, ReLU, sigmoid output) trained on one layer's activations at the statement's final token. It is trained on five topics and tested on the held-out sixth. Models: OPT-6.7b and LLaMA2-7b (32 layers, 4,096 units). A second set has 245 statements generated by OPT-6.7b and labelled by three human judges.
  - What it establishes:
    - Hidden activations carry a truth signal that transfers to a topic held out from training (Sec. 5, Tables 1-2).
    - Baselines that use the same model's outputs stay near chance. These are few-shot prompting scored by the "true"/"false" token probabilities, and an "It-is-true" baseline that compares the probabilities of the statement after "It is true that" and "It is false that" (Sec. 5, Table 1).
    - Raw sentence probability depends on sentence length and word frequency, illustrated on 14 statements that are not in the dataset (Sec. 6, Table 5).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 1 (OPT-6.7b, average accuracy on held-out topics, p. 6): SAPLMA by layer: last 0.6449, 28th 0.6507, 24th 0.6886, 20th 0.7098, middle 0.6515. Baselines: BERT 0.5434, 3-shot 0.5374, 5-shot 0.5370, It-is-true 0.5593. Table 2 (LLaMA2-7b, p. 6): best is the 16th layer at 0.8298. Table 3 (OPT-generated statements, threshold 0.5, p. 7): best SAPLMA is the 28th layer at 0.6362 accuracy (AUC 0.7614); 3-shot 0.5041, 5-shot 0.5125. Table 4 (threshold set on a held-out 30% of the generated statements, tested on the other 70%): 28th layer 0.7134. GPT-4, prompted on the same generated statements, reached 84.4% (Sec. 5, p. 8).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): The task is true vs. false, not certain vs. unsure. Only English was tested. Activations were taken from isolated sentences, not from inside longer generations (Sec. 8, p. 9). The best layer depends on the LLM (Sec. 5). Generated statements have less clear-cut truth values (Sec. 5).
  - Relation to the review: Bears on RQ3's geometry candidate and on locating errors at decision points (RQ2). The model's internal state carries correctness information that its output probabilities do not expose. Supportive, in a narrow setting.
  - The hinge: The model reads and classifies isolated, mostly templated factual statements. The review concerns errors that arise at decision points inside generated answers.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "A classifier trained on an LLM's hidden activations predicted whether a statement is true more accurately than the model's own probabilities for the statement."
  - Source passage: "SAPLMA classifications of truth/falsehood significantly outperform simple sentence probability" (Sec. 1, p. 2). The quantitative comparison with the model's probabilities is Table 1 (OPT-6.7b): 0.7098 against 0.5593 for It-is-true and about 0.537 for few-shot. Raw sentence probability, the product of token probabilities, is compared only qualitatively on 14 statements (Table 5), and no accuracy is reported for it.
  - Scope: The probability baselines exist only for OPT-6.7b; LLaMA2-7b was evaluated with SAPLMA alone. Topics are held out, data are balanced, and results are averaged over three training runs.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "On simple true/false statements from a held-out topic, a small classifier trained on OPT-6.7b's 20th-layer activations averaged 71.0% accuracy. Baselines built from the same model's outputs stayed near chance: 55.9% when comparing its probabilities for 'It is true that X' and 'It is false that X', and about 54% with few-shot prompting. With LLaMA2-7b the classifier averaged 83.0% [azaria2023internal]."
- Bib notes: From the ACL Anthology BibTeX (azaria-mitchell-2023-internal): authors, title ({LLM} protected), booktitle, pp. 967-976, Singapore, ACL, DOI. Crossref confirms the DOI, pages and authors.

### burns2023discovering
- Source of record: https://openreview.net/forum?id=ETKGuby0hcs (ICLR 2023; OpenReview venue "ICLR 2023 poster", venueid ICLR.cc/2023/Conference; BibTeX booktitle "The Eleventh International Conference on Learning Representations"). Access: full text (arXiv v2, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.03827v2, 2 Mar 2024; v1, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.03827v1, checked for Table 1 and the Sec. 3.2.2 numbers, which are identical). The OpenReview PDF returned HTTP 403 and was not opened.
