### kuhn2023semantic
- Source of record: https://openreview.net/forum?id=VD-AYtP0dve (ICLR 2023, The Eleventh International Conference on Learning Representations; in-person oral, "top 25%" per https://iclr.cc/virtual/2023/oral/12609; no page numbers or DOI). Access: full text (arXiv v3, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.09664v3, 15 Apr 2023, carrying the ICLR 2023 camera-ready running head); the OpenReview PDF was not opened (challenge page, not bypassed).
- Retraction: none found. There is no Crossref record (ICLR papers on OpenReview have no DOI); the plain and retraction-filtered Crossref queries returned only unrelated titles. The arXiv abs page (v3) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: free-form question answering with unmodified, not fine-tuned OPT models of 2.7B, 6.7B, 13B and 30B parameters; headline results use 30B (Sec. 6, p.7). Datasets: CoQA, open-book, development split (about 8000 questions), and TriviaQA, closed-book, a subset of 8000 training-split questions (Sec. 6, p.7). Ten sampled answers per question; an answer counts as correct if its Rouge-L with the reference exceeds 0.3 (Sec. 6, p.7; Sec. 6.1, p.8; App. B, p.16). Meanings are clustered by bidirectional entailment, judged in the context of the question by DeBERTa-large fine-tuned on MNLI (Sec. 4, p.5). Evaluation: AUROC for predicting whether the model's answer is correct.
  - What it establishes:
    - Entropy over meaning clusters (semantic entropy) predicted answer correctness better than predictive entropy over sampled token sequences, with or without length normalisation, better than lexical similarity, and much better than p(True) on TriviaQA with OPT-30B; on CoQA the advantage was significant at the larger model sizes (Sec. 6.1, p.8; Fig. 1, p.2; Fig. 2, p.7).
    - The advantage grows with model size and with the number of samples (Fig. 1b, p.2; Fig. 3a, p.9).
    - Questions answered incorrectly produced more distinct meanings among the samples than questions answered correctly (Table 2, p.8).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 2 (p.8; OPT-30B, 10 samples): mean number of semantically distinct answers, correct vs incorrect, CoQA 1.27 vs 1.77, TriviaQA 1.89 vs 3.89; AUROC of semantic entropy 0.77 (CoQA) and 0.83 (TriviaQA), against 0.66 and 0.79 for the count of distinct answers. Table 6 (p.17; TriviaQA, Rouge-L > 0.3): semantic entropy 0.828, normalised entropy 0.802, accuracy 0.506. Table 7 (p.18; CoQA, Rouge-L > 0.3): 0.7672 vs 0.7533, accuracy 0.8239. The comparisons with plain predictive entropy, lexical similarity and p(True) appear only as bar charts (Figs. 1-2).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): semantic entropy only partly corrects for unequal token importance, because the likelihoods of non-key tokens still enter the cluster probabilities (Sec. 4.1, p.6; App. A.1, p.14). The entailment clustering is soft, sometimes merging answers that are not quite equivalent, and so not strictly transitive (App. A.1, p.14). On CoQA at high sampling temperature (T = 1.5), normalised entropy beat semantic entropy and clustering accuracy fell to 61% (App. A.3 and Fig. 4, p.15). The method does not protect against a model capable of deception (Sec. 7, p.9). Only question answering was studied; summarisation is left to future work (Sec. 7, p.9). Their p(True) baseline was 10-shot with OPT up to 30B, against 20-shot with a 52B model in the original (Sec. 6, p.8).
  - Relation to the review: RQ2 (hardness); a precedent. Scoring uncertainty over meanings rather than over every token predicts which answers are wrong, which fits the review's view that risk sits in the choices that change the answer rather than spreading evenly over tokens. The authors' own remark that the method still weights non-key tokens states the review's key versus non-key distinction from the uncertainty side.
  - The hinge: the method scores whole short answers to single questions by comparing several samples; the review asks how error risk is distributed over the positions of long outputs.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Semantic entropy estimates uncertainty over meanings rather than token sequences, by clustering sampled answers that entail each other, so that variation in wording is not counted as uncertainty; it predicted the correctness of answers to questions better than token-level baselines."
  - Source passage: "more predictive of model accuracy on question answering data sets than comparable baselines" (Abstract, p.1). The clustering by bidirectional entailment, with meaning judged relative to the question, is in Sec. 4 (p.5) and Algorithm 1 (p.15). Pooling the probabilities of answers with the same meaning before taking the entropy is Eq. (2)-(3) and Table 1 (pp.4-5).
  - Scope: free-form QA (TriviaQA, CoQA) with OPT 2.7B-30B. The baselines are predictive entropy computed from the probabilities of the sampled token sequences (with and without length normalisation), lexical similarity, and p(True); "token-level baselines" misnames them. The gains are clearest for the larger models; on CoQA they are significant only at the larger sizes, and they depend on the sampling temperature (best at 0.5).
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "Semantic entropy estimates uncertainty over meanings rather than token sequences: it clusters sampled answers that entail each other in both directions, so that variation in wording is not counted as uncertainty. On free-form question answering (TriviaQA and CoQA) with OPT models of 2.7B to 30B parameters, it predicted whether an answer was correct better than entropy computed from the probabilities of the sampled sequences, with or without length normalisation, and better than lexical similarity and p(True), with the clearest gains for the larger models (AUROC 0.83 on TriviaQA with OPT-30B) [kuhn2023semantic]."
- Bib notes: title, author order and affiliation checked on the arXiv v3 title page; venue and presentation type on https://iclr.cc/virtual/2023/poster/11013 and /oral/12609 (authors Lorenz Kuhn, Yarin Gal, Sebastian Farquhar; OpenReview id VD-AYtP0dve). The booktitle follows the form "The Eleventh International Conference on Learning Representations" used for ICLR 2023 (ICLR 2023 was the eleventh ICLR; the EMNLP 2023 SelfCheckGPT paper cites it in this form). No page numbers or DOI exist. Minor internal inconsistency in the paper: entailment-classifier accuracy on CoQA is 95.5% in Sec. 4 (p.5) but 95.3% in App. B.2 (p.17); TriviaQA is 92.7% in both. Neither figure is used here.

### farquhar2024detecting
- Source of record: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07421-0 (Nature, vol. 630, no. 8017, pp. 625-630, 2024; received 17 July 2023, accepted 12 April 2024, published online 19 June 2024, issue of 20 June 2024; open access, CC BY 4.0; DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07421-0). Access: full text (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07421-0.pdf, main text and Methods; the article page was also checked).
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref record 10.1038/s41586-024-07421-0 has no update-to, updated-by or relation entries, and the Nature article page shows no correction, retraction or Matters Arising notice.
- Card:
  - Setting: five free-form datasets answered without their context passages: TriviaQA, SQuAD 1.1, BioASQ, NQ-Open and SVAMP (math word problems), with 400 training and 400 test questions sampled from each (p.626; Methods, PDF p.8). Sentence-length answers (mean 96 characters) from LLaMA 2 Chat 7B, 13B and 70B, Falcon Instruct 7B and 40B, and Mistral Instruct 7B (p.626). Ten generations per question (p.628). GPT-3.5 judges bidirectional entailment, and GPT-4 judges correctness against the reference answers (Methods). Plus FactualBio: GPT-4 biographies of 21 people, decomposed into 150 factual claims, of which 45 were incorrect; here a discrete variant that needs no token probabilities is used (pp.628-629; Methods, PDF p.10).
  - What it establishes:
    - Confabulations are defined as answers that are wrong and arbitrary, that is, sensitive to irrelevant details such as the random seed. The authors separate them from consistent errors caused by erroneous training data, from lying in pursuit of reward, and from systematic failures of reasoning or generalisation (p.625).
    - Semantic entropy predicted which answers were incorrect better than naive entropy (the entropy of length-normalised token-sequence probabilities), P(True), and a supervised embedding-regression baseline, on average over the 30 dataset-model pairs and in every model family (Fig. 2, p.627; pp.627-628). The discrete variant performed similarly (p.627).
    - The measured quantity is the prediction of incorrect answers. The authors read Fig. 2 as a lower bound on confabulation detection, because the incorrect answers also include errors that semantic entropy is not suited to detect (p.628).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): averaged over the 30 combinations of tasks and models, AUROC 0.790 for semantic entropy against 0.691 for naive entropy, 0.698 for P(True) and 0.687 for embedding regression; semantic entropy stays between 0.78 and 0.81 across model families and sizes (pp.627-628). FactualBio: 45 of 150 claims incorrect (Methods, PDF p.10).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): the method does not guarantee factuality and does not help when outputs are systematically bad (p.625). The Fig. 2 evaluation also counts mistakes the method is not suited to, such as consistent errors learned from training data (p.628). The Discussion says the method does not directly address confident errors produced by training objectives, systematic reasoning errors, or systematically misleading outputs, which need separate handling (p.629). Clustering can be too fine relative to the reference answer, as in the date-versus-year example (Table 1, row 4, p.628). Semantic equivalence is harder to apply to passages that state many propositions, and in the biography setting the question-generation step is the main source of error (p.628; Methods).
  - Relation to the review: RQ3 (dependence); supports and qualifies. The paper draws the line the review relies on: a check built on disagreement between samples sees only errors that vary across samples, and errors repeated across samples are invisible to it by design. It also qualifies the review: in these short-answer settings the authors suggest that confabulations are a principal category of errors, because semantic entropy outperformed a supervised detector that can see other kinds of error (p.628). The review should not imply that most errors are shared across samples.
  - The hinge: the evaluation asks whether a whole short answer, or a single biography claim, is wrong; it does not measure how often errors repeat across samples or across models, which is the quantity the review's dependence argument turns on.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Semantic entropy detected confabulations, arbitrary wrong answers, across datasets and models, but by design it does not catch errors the model makes consistently."
  - Source passage: "it does not help when LLM outputs are systematically bad" (p.625). The paper also says the evaluated mistakes include consistent errors learned from training data, for which semantic entropy is not suited (p.628); that the method does not directly address systematic reasoning errors or confident errors produced by training objectives (Discussion, p.629); and that semantic entropy is designed to detect outputs with arbitrary meaning (Methods, PDF p.8).
  - Scope: confabulation detection was measured as predicting incorrect answers on five QA and math datasets with six open models of 7B-70B parameters, and incorrect claims in GPT-4 biographies.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. If a number is wanted: "...across five question-answering and math datasets and six open models (average AUROC 0.790, against 0.691 for entropy over token sequences) [farquhar2024detecting]."
- Bib notes: authors (Sebastian Farquhar, Jannik Kossen, Lorenz Kuhn, Yarin Gal; the first three contributed equally), title, journal, volume 630, issue 8017, pages 625-630 and DOI verified against the Crossref record and the Springer Nature RIS export; the dates were checked on the article page. The title is kept in the journal's sentence case.

### kadavath2022language
- Source of record: https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221 (arXiv preprint, Anthropic; v1 July 2022, latest v4 21 Nov 2022). No peer-reviewed version was found: the arXiv record has no journal reference, Crossref has no record, DBLP lists it under CoRR only (search result; the DBLP page itself is behind a bot check), and the venue papers in this batch that cite it (ICLR 2023, EMNLP 2023, ACL 2024, Nature 2024) all cite the arXiv preprint. Access: full text (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.05221v4).
- Retraction: none found. No Crossref record; the plain and retraction-filtered queries returned only unrelated titles. The arXiv abs page (v4) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: Anthropic pretrained language models with 800M, 3B, 12B and 52B parameters, trained on 850B tokens; RLHF policies appear only in Sec. 3.3 (Sec. 1.2, pp.5-6). Calibration was measured on BIG Bench multiple-choice tasks, MMLU, TruthfulQA, LogiQA and QuALITY; self-evaluation and P(IK) on TriviaQA, Lambada, GSM8k, Codex HumanEval, arithmetic and Python function synthesis (Sec. 1.2, pp.5-6).
  - What it establishes:
    - Calibration: with lettered answer options shown in the prompt, large models are well calibrated on diverse multiple-choice questions, and calibration improves with model size and with few-shot prompting (Sec. 2, pp.7-8; Fig. 4). In a True/False format the 52B model is well calibrated except near the tails, where it is overconfident (Sec. 3.2 and Fig. 8, p.10). Replacing an option with "none of the above" harms both accuracy and calibration (Sec. 3.1, pp.8-10). RLHF policies look miscalibrated, but a single temperature of T = 2.5 largely fixes this on three evaluations (Sec. 3.3, p.11).
    - P(True): models can judge whether their own sampled answers are correct, though this is harder than judging answers written by others. Zero-shot P(True) is poorly calibrated, and few-shot P(True) is well calibrated. Showing the model several of its own samples improves the judgement, less so on long-form tasks (Sec. 1.1, p.4; Secs. 4.1-4.2, pp.11-13).
    - P(IK): a value head fine-tuned on TriviaQA predicts whether the model will answer a question correctly and is well calibrated in distribution. It generalises only partly to other tasks, where it is often poorly calibrated, mostly underconfident (Secs. 5.1-5.2, pp.14-16; Fig. 13, p.15; Table 1, p.18).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 1 (p.18; 52B; AUROC of P(IK)): trained on TriviaQA only: TriviaQA 0.864, Mixed-Arithmetic 0.928, LAMBADA 0.606, Python Func Synth. 0.687, GSM8K 0.624; trained on all tasks except GSM8K: 0.873, 0.987, 0.853, 0.881, 0.752. Table 2 (p.21): the model-specific signal in P(IK) is a difference of only about 6%. P(True) results are given only in figures (Figs. 1, 10, 11; App. C).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): the study focuses on pretrained models; fine-tuned models such as RLHF policies will be less well calibrated, and some methods may work less well for them (Sec. 6.1, p.23). The analysis does not separate the truth from what humans say, and it does not address learned deception (Sec. 6.1, pp.22-23). Only five sampling-based datasets were studied, so the findings on scaling and generalisation are limited in power and scope (Sec. 6.1, p.23). P(IK) may partly capture the difficulty of a question rather than the model's own knowledge; this is only partly addressed (footnote 2, p.3; Sec. 5.5, pp.20-21).
  - Relation to the review: RQ2 (hardness); consistent with it. The models' own estimates separate the questions and answers they get right from those they get wrong, so error risk is concentrated in identifiable items rather than spread evenly. That P(True) improves when the model sees its other samples links to RQ3: agreement among samples carries information about correctness.
  - The hinge: confidence is estimated for a whole question or a whole sampled answer on short-answer and code tasks; the review asks about risk at individual positions inside long generations.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Large models are well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions presented in a suitable format, can evaluate whether their own samples are true (P(True)), and can be trained to predict whether they know the answer (P(IK))."
  - Source passage: "larger models are well-calibrated on diverse multiple choice and true/false questions" (Abstract, p.1); the sentence continues with the condition that the questions are given in the right format. P(True): Secs. 4.1-4.2 (pp.11-13). P(IK): Sec. 5 and Table 1 (pp.14-18).
  - Scope: Anthropic's own pretrained models up to 52B parameters. Calibration requires a format with the options visible; P(True) is well calibrated only few-shot; P(IK) generalises only partly and is poorly calibrated out of distribution.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "In Anthropic's pretrained language models of up to 52B parameters, larger models were well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions when the answer options were shown in a suitable format; models could estimate the probability that their own sampled answer was correct (P(True)), with good calibration only when prompted with a few examples; and models fine-tuned to predict whether they know the answer (P(IK)) did so well on the training distribution but generalized only partly to new tasks, where their calibration was poor [kadavath2022language]."
- Bib notes: all 36 authors, in the order and spelling of the arXiv metadata and the PDF byline (including Hatfield-Dodds, El-Showk, DasSarma, Tran-Johnson); title from the arXiv v4 title page; entered as @misc with the arXiv DOI and note "Preprint".

### manakul2023selfcheckgpt
- Source of record: https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.557/ (Proceedings of the 2023 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing, Singapore, December 2023, pp. 9004-9017; DOI 10.18653/v1/2023.emnlp-main.557). Access: full text (https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.557.pdf; arXiv v3 https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08896, comment "EMNLP 2023 (main conference)", checked for metadata).
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref record 10.18653/v1/2023.emnlp-main.557 has no update or relation entries; the ACL Anthology page lists no erratum; the arXiv abs page (v3) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: GPT-3 (text-davinci-003) writes Wikipedia-style passages about 238 WikiBio individuals (1908 sentences); each sentence is labelled by hand as major-inaccurate, minor-inaccurate or accurate (Sec. 6, p.9008). The main response is generated at temperature 0, and N = 20 further samples at temperature 1.0 (Sec. 7, p.9009). Five ways of measuring consistency with the samples: BERTScore, multiple-choice question answering (MQAG), an n-gram model, NLI (DeBERTa-v3-large fine-tuned on MNLI), and prompting an LLM (GPT-3 or ChatGPT) (Sec. 5, pp.9006-9008). The baselines are GPT-3's own token probabilities and entropies (grey-box) and the probabilities of proxy LLMs such as LLaMA-30B.
  - What it establishes:
    - The premise: if the model has knowledge of a concept, its sampled responses are likely to be similar and contain consistent facts, while for hallucinated facts they are likely to diverge and contradict one another (Abstract, p.9004; Sec. 1, p.9005).
    - Consistency with the samples detected non-factual sentences better than GPT-3's own token probabilities and entropies; the prompting and NLI variants were best (Table 2, p.9010; Sec. 7.1, pp.9009-9011). Passage-level scores also correlated better with human judgements (Table 2; Sec. 7.2, p.9011).
    - GPT-3's own token probabilities were already strong baselines: factual sentences tended to contain more likely, lower-entropy tokens (Sec. 3.1, p.9006; Sec. 7.1, observation 1, p.9009).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 2 (p.9010), sentence-level AUC-PR for non-factual sentences: random 72.96; GPT-3 Avg(-logp) 83.21; GPT-3 Max(-logp) 87.51; SelfCheckGPT with NLI 92.50; with Prompt 93.42. Passage-level Pearson correlation with human scores: Prompt 78.32. Labels (Sec. 6, p.9008): 761 (39.9%) major-inaccurate, 631 (33.1%) minor-inaccurate, 516 (27.0%) accurate sentences.
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): the passages are mostly about individuals in WikiBio; factuality is judged per sentence, although one sentence can mix factual and non-factual information; the best variant (Prompt) is computationally heavy (Limitations, p.9012). Less capable models (GPT-3 text-curie-001, LLaMA) failed to perform the prompted consistency check (Sec. 5.5, p.9008).
  - Relation to the review: RQ3 (dependence); a precedent. The method is built on the assumption that hallucinations vary across samples, so it sees only the part of the error that differs between samples. By construction, a falsehood reproduced in every sample would score as consistent; this is our inference and is not tested in the paper. Caution for RQ2: about 73% of the sentences in this dataset were labelled inaccurate, so the work is not evidence that errors are sparse.
  - The hinge: detection works sentence by sentence on GPT-3 biographies and uses disagreement among samples as its signal; the review asks how often errors agree across samples.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "SelfCheckGPT flags hallucinated sentences by checking whether they are consistent across sampled responses, on the premise that facts a model knows are reproduced consistently while hallucinations diverge."
  - Source passage: "for hallucinated facts, stochastically sampled responses are likely to diverge and contradict one another" (Abstract, p.9004). The matching half of the premise, that knowledge of a concept makes sampled responses similar and factually consistent, is in the same paragraph and restated in Sec. 1 (p.9005). The sentence-level scoring against N samples is defined in Sec. 5 (p.9006).
  - Scope: the premise is stated as a likelihood, not a certainty, and it was tested on GPT-3 (text-davinci-003) biographies with 20 samples per passage.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE.
- Bib notes: title, authors, booktitle, pages, month, address, publisher and DOI from the ACL Anthology .bib export, cross-checked with Crossref. The Anthology export gives "Gales, Mark"; the PDF byline reads "Mark J. F. Gales", which the bib follows (the convention of part J2). The Nature paper (farquhar2024detecting, ref. 41) lists SelfCheckGPT under "Findings of EMNLP 2023"; the Anthology places it in the main proceedings (2023.emnlp-main.557), as does the arXiv comment. The main-conference venue is used.

### duan2024shifting
- Source of record: https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.276/ (Proceedings of the 62nd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics, Volume 1: Long Papers, Bangkok, August 2024, pp. 5050-5063; DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.276). Access: full text (https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.276.pdf; arXiv v3 https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.01379v3, comment "To appear in ACL 2024"; the cited passages are identical).
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref record 10.18653/v1/2024.acl-long.276 has no update or relation entries; the ACL Anthology page lists no erratum; the arXiv abs page (v3) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: free-form QA on CoQA, TriviaQA, SciQ, MedQA and MedMCQA. Pretrained OPT 2.7B, 6.7B, 13B and 30B and LLaMA 7B and 13B; instruction-tuned Vicuna 13B and 33B, WizardLM 13B and LLaMA-2-13B-chat (Sec. 5.1, pp.5055-5056; App. A-B, p.5061). The greedy answer is scored for correctness (Rouge-L > 0.5 in the main results, or sentence similarity); 5 or 10 sampled answers are used for uncertainty; evaluation by AUROC (Sec. 5.1, p.5056). A token's relevance is one minus the similarity between the answer with and without that token, measured by a RoBERTa-large cross-encoder (Sec. 3.2, Eq. 2, pp.5052-5053). The word attention in the title refers to this relevance reweighting, not to transformer attention.
  - What it establishes:
    - In an analysis of OPT-13B on CoQA (10 samples per question), most generated tokens had low relevance, and low-relevance tokens and sentences carried most of the uncertainty (Sec. 3.4, pp.5053-5054; Figs. 2-3, p.5053). For tokens, the uncertainty proportions are summed within each relevance bin because low-relevance tokens are the majority (Sec. 3.4, p.5054), so the dominance is in aggregate and partly reflects their number.
    - SAR combines relevance-weighted token entropies with relevance-weighted sentence probabilities. It gave a higher AUROC than lexical similarity, predictive entropy, its length-normalised form and semantic entropy in most settings (Table 1, p.5055; Fig. 4, p.5055; Sec. 5.3, p.5057; Table 4, p.5057).
    - Reweighting tokens alone (TOKENSAR) gave small gains; most of the improvement came from the sentence-level component and from combining the two (Table 1, p.5055; Sec. 5.2, p.5057; Table 6, p.5063).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): Table 1 (p.5055; instruction-tuned models, Rouge-L 0.5), averages: LS 0.555, PE 0.675, LN-PE 0.644, SE 0.653, TOKENSAR 0.678 (+2.5% over SE), SENTSAR 0.727, SAR 0.724 (+7.1%). Sec. 5.2 (p.5057): on OPT-30b with CoQA, TOKENSAR and SENTSAR each reach 0.723 AUROC and SAR 0.748. Table 6 (p.5063; pretrained models, Rouge-L 0.3, the threshold of kuhn2023semantic), averages: LN-PE 0.712, SE 0.731, TOKENSAR 0.715, SAR 0.742; on LLaMA-13b with TriviaQA, SAR 0.716 against SE 0.758.
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): the method needs access to token logits, which limits black-box use, and the sentence-similarity computations add latency (Sec. 8 Limitations, p.5058).
  - Relation to the review: RQ1 and RQ2; supports the review as a precedent. Token-level uncertainty is dominated by tokens that carry little of the answer's meaning, so a uniform per-token treatment misplaces risk. This parallels the review's two-rate argument on the uncertainty side.
  - The hinge: relevance is defined by how much a token changes the meaning of a short answer, not by whether errors occur at it; the paper does not test whether the high-relevance tokens are where errors arise.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Tokens differ in how much they carry the meaning of a generation, and reweighting token-level uncertainty by each token's relevance improved uncertainty estimates."
  - Source passage: "Irrelevant tokens and sentences dominate the total volume of uncertainty quantification." (Fig. 3 caption, p.5053). The finding that most tokens have low relevance is in Sec. 3.4 (p.5053); the comparisons are in Table 1 (p.5055) and Table 6 (p.5063).
  - Scope: short free-form QA answers; open models up to 33B parameters. The first half of the intended sentence holds. For the second half, the robust gains belong to the combined token-and-sentence method (SAR); token-level reweighting alone was only 0.003 above predictive entropy on average for the instruction-tuned models (0.678 vs 0.675) and below semantic entropy for the pretrained models at Rouge-L 0.3 (0.715 vs 0.731). The margins depend on the correctness threshold.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "Duan et al. measured each token's relevance as the change in an answer's meaning when the token is removed.
