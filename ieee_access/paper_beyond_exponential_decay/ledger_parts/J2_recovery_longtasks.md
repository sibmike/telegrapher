### kamoi2024selfcorrect
- Source of record: https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.78/ (Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics, 2024, vol. 12, pp. 1417-1440, MIT Press, DOI 10.1162/tacl_a_00713; published 11/2024). Access: full text (https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.78.pdf; arXiv abs https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297, v3, checked for metadata).
- Retraction: none found. Crossref record 10.1162/tacl_a_00713 has no update, relation or retraction field. arXiv abs (v3, journal-ref TACL 12:1417-1440) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: A critical survey of LLM self-correction, which the authors define as refining an LLM's responses with LLMs during inference, possibly helped by external tools or knowledge (Sec. 2, p.1418). Prior studies are sorted by research question (RQ1: inherent capabilities only; RQ2: with external information; RQ3: compared with other methods) and by framework (realistic or unrealistic; fair or unfair initial responses) (Sec. 3, pp.1421-1422). The literature covered runs to 2024 (submitted 5/2024, revised 8/2024, p.1417).
  - What it establishes:
    - Four summary findings (Sec. 1, p.1417; restated per RQ on p.1418). No prior work shows successful self-correction with feedback from prompted LLMs in general tasks (Sec. 4). Self-correction works well where reliable external feedback is available (Sec. 5.1). Large-scale fine-tuning enables it (Sec. 5.2). Some tasks are exceptionally suited to it, for example tasks whose responses decompose into parts that are easy to verify (Sec. 4).
    - Many positive results used oracle information (ground-truth answers) or deliberately weak prompts for the initial response, and both over-evaluate self-correction (Table 4: RCI, Reflexion, CAI Revisions, Self-Refine; Sec. 4, p.1423). The authors therefore conclude that no major work shows success with self-generated feedback under fair settings in general tasks (Sec. 4, p.1423).
    - The bottleneck is generating the feedback. The premise that recognising errors is easier than avoiding them holds only for tasks whose verification is exceptionally easy (Sec. 7, p.1426).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): this is a survey and reports almost no numbers of its own. The one quantitative statement relevant here: many supervised fine-tuning methods for feedback generation use more than 100K training instances (Sec. 5.2 and Table 6, p.1425).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): the survey covers inference-time self-correction by the same model. Cross-model correction is excluded from RQ1 and RQ2 (Sec. 3.2, p.1422; Sec. 9, p.1427). Most intrinsic self-correction studies used simple feedback prompts, so there is room for improvement, and there may be unexplored tasks where intrinsic self-correction works (Sec. 11, p.1428). Fine-tuning with small training sets is unexplored (p.1418; Sec. 11, p.1428).
  - Relation to the review: bears on RQ3 (dependence) and is consistent with it. Feedback generated by the model itself did not reliably catch its own errors, while feedback from a reliable outside source did. This fits the review's point that a check correlated with the generator adds less than an independent one. The work surveys published evidence, so it qualifies the picture and measures nothing itself.
  - The hinge: the survey asks whether published experiments show improvement over best-possible initial responses. The review asks how far errors are correlated within an answer and across samples. The survey does not measure error correlation.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "A critical survey found no prior work that shows successful self-correction from feedback generated by prompted LLMs themselves in general tasks, while self-correction works with reliable external feedback or with fine-tuning on suitable data."
  - Source passage: "no prior work shows successful self-correction with feedback from prompted LLMs in general tasks" (Sec. 1, p.1417). Sec. 4 (p.1423) restates the conclusion more cautiously: it covers major published work under fair settings, meaning best-possible initial responses and no oracle information. The abstract (p.1417) excludes tasks exceptionally suited to self-correction. External feedback: Sec. 1 (p.1417) and Sec. 5.1 (p.1424). Fine-tuning: the summary finding is that fine-tuning at large scale enables self-correction (p.1417). The RQ2 summary adds that it works when large training data is available and is unexplored for small data (p.1418).
  - Scope: published work up to 2024; inference-time self-correction by the same model. "General tasks" excludes tasks with easily verified answers, such as decomposable responses, constrained generation or Game of 24 (Sec. 4, p.1424; Sec. 5.1, pp.1424-1425). The fine-tuning finding is tied to large training sets, not to "suitable data".
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "A 2024 critical survey found no prior work showing successful self-correction with feedback from prompted LLMs in general tasks under fair settings, except in tasks exceptionally suited to it, such as those whose answers decompose into easily verified parts; self-correction worked where reliable external feedback was available, and large-scale fine-tuning enabled it [kamoi2024selfcorrect]."
- Bib notes: fields taken from the ACL Anthology export (https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.78.bib): five authors in order, title, journal, volume 12, pages 1417--1440, year 2024, publisher MIT Press (Cambridge, MA), DOI. These were cross-checked against Crossref (same volume and pages, same author order, published 2024-11-04) and the PDF footer on p.1417. Capitals are protected as {LLMs}, and as {A} after the question mark. The Anthology writes {LLM}s, which renders the same.

### kumar2025score
- Source of record: https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/871ac99fdc5282d0301934d23945ebaa-Abstract-Conference.html (ICLR 2025, proceedings pp. 54523-54549). The iclr.cc virtual page https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/oral/31899 lists it as an oral; OpenReview forum CjwERcAU7w. Access: full text (ICLR camera-ready https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/871ac99fdc5282d0301934d23945ebaa-Paper-Conference.pdf; arXiv v2 https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.12917v2). The abstract, Tables 1-4 and the Sec. 6.1 numbers are identical in both. Verdict on venue: ICLR 2025, as assigned.
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref bibliographic query returns no record for this paper (ICLR proceedings have no DOIs), and the records it does return carry no relation fields. arXiv abs (v2) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: Google DeepMind. SCoRe is two-stage, multi-turn online RL (REINFORCE with a KL penalty). It trains one model to give a first attempt and then a self-corrected second attempt, using only data the model generates itself (Sec. 1, p.2; Sec. 5, pp.6-8). Training uses an oracle reward from an answer checker; no oracle is available at test time (Sec. 3, p.4). MATH: Gemini 1.5 Flash, tested on MATH500, with 4,500 MATH test problems added to the training set following Lightman et al. Code: Gemini 1.0 Pro trained on MBPP and tested on HumanEval, plus the offline repair task MBPP-R (Sec. 6, p.8). Evaluation uses greedy decoding and one round of self-correction, i.e. two attempts (Sec. 6, pp.8-9). Baselines: Self-Refine (prompting), multi-turn STaR, and Pair-SFT after Welleck et al. (Sec. 6, p.9).
  - What it establishes:
    - An untrained second attempt hurt the base model on MATH: it turned more correct answers into incorrect ones (15.8%) than incorrect into correct (4.6%) (Table 1, p.4; Table 2, p.9).
    - SFT on offline correction traces (STaR, Pair-SFT) gave small or negative self-correction gains. The causes were distribution shift and collapse to non-correcting behaviour (Sec. 4, pp.4-6; Table 1, p.4).
    - SCoRe gave positive intrinsic self-correction on both benchmarks (Tables 2-3, p.9). Ablations show that multi-turn training, Stage I and reward shaping each contribute (Table 4, p.10).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): MATH500 with Gemini 1.5 Flash (Table 2, p.9). Base model: Acc.@t1 52.6%, Acc.@t2 41.4%, Δ(t1,t2) -11.2%. Self-Refine Δ -1.0%, STaR Δ 0.4%, Pair-SFT Δ 1.8%. SCoRe: 60.0% → 64.4%, Δ 4.4% (Δi→c 5.8%, Δc→i 1.4%). The text says SCoRe raises Δ by 15.6% and Acc.@t2 by 23.0% over the base model (Sec. 6.1, p.9). HumanEval with Gemini 1.0 Pro (Table 3, p.9). Base model: 53.7% → 56.7%, Δ 3.0%. Self-Refine Δ -1.2%, Pair-SFT Δ -1.8%. SCoRe: 52.4% → 64.6%, Δ 12.2%. MBPP-R is 47.3% for the base model and 60.6% for SCoRe. The abstract gives gains of 15.6% (MATH) and 9.1% (HumanEval) (p.1). The Table 3 difference is 9.2 points, and the text says "9% higher" (p.9).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): Stages I and II need separate runs, and adding a critique between turns might help (Sec. 7, p.10). The model was trained for two attempts. Over more attempts, SCoRe gains only slightly and then plateaus, and better scaling is left to future work (App. A.3, p.17). A three-attempt extension on Gemma 2 2B is in App. A.1 (p.16).
  - Relation to the review: bears on RQ3. An untrained second pass by the same model is not an independent check: on MATH it undid more correct answers than it fixed. Training on the model's own mistakes changes that. This is a precedent that recovery can be learned. It is not evidence that errors are independent.
  - The hinge: here, correction is one explicitly prompted second attempt over a whole answer, trained against a ground-truth reward. The review asks how errors at key decisions propagate within one generated answer and across samples.
  - Other finding a reviewer may raise: SCoRe also raised first-attempt accuracy on MATH (52.6% to 60.0%) and lowered it slightly on HumanEval (53.7% to 52.4%), so the comparisons of Δ start from different first attempts (Tables 2-3, p.9).
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Training self-correction with multi-turn reinforcement learning on the model's own correction traces raised self-correction gains on MATH and HumanEval."
  - Source passage: "improving the base models' self-correction by 15.6% and 9.1% respectively on MATH and HumanEval" (Abstract, p.1). The abstract and Sec. 5 (pp.6-8) say training uses the model's own on-policy correction traces. Values are in Tables 2-3 (p.9).
  - Scope: Gemini 1.5 Flash on MATH500; Gemini 1.0 Pro on HumanEval after training on MBPP; one round of correction; a reward from an answer checker or unit tests during training only; greedy decoding.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. If the sentence should carry numbers, this version is fully supported: "SCoRe trains a model with multi-turn reinforcement learning on its own two-attempt correction traces, rewarded by an answer checker during training only; the accuracy change from first to second attempt rose from -11.2 to +4.4 points on MATH500 with Gemini 1.5 Flash, and from 3.0 to 12.2 points on HumanEval with Gemini 1.0 Pro [kumar2025score]."
- Bib notes: pages 54523--54549, year 2025 and the 18 authors in order are from the proceedings.iclr.cc BibTeX export. The export and the iclr.cc page give "JD Co-Reyes" and "Lei Zhang". The camera-ready byline (p.1) gives "JD Co-Reyes" and "Lei M Zhang", and arXiv gives "John D Co-Reyes" and "Lei M Zhang". The bib uses "Co-Reyes, John D." and "Zhang, Lei M." so that IEEE prints the initials correctly. The booktitle follows the other ledger parts.

### ye2025physics22
- Source of record: https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/c239bac713017b0b2257b7622bf8aab3-Abstract-Conference.html (ICLR 2025, poster; proceedings pp. 78136-78147). The OpenReview search API lists forum zpDGwcmMV4 as "ICLR 2025 Poster". The ICLR camera-ready is a 12-page "[Extended abstract]" that leaves Results 7-8 and several figures to the full version. The authors note that the full paper went through ICLR review (Sec. 5, p.10). Access: full text (ICLR PDF https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/c239bac713017b0b2257b7622bf8aab3-Paper-Conference.pdf; full version arXiv v1 https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.16293). The passages and numbers cited agree between the two. Verdict on venue: ICLR 2025, as assigned.
- Retraction: none found. Crossref returns the SSRN version (10.2139/ssrn.5250631, 2025), which has no update or relation field. The arXiv abs page (v1 only) shows no withdrawal. It carries an arXiv admin note about text overlap with Part 2.1 (arXiv:2407.20311).
- Card:
  - Setting: iGSM is a program-generated, GSM8K-like synthetic dataset. Arithmetic is done modulo 23 and common-sense content is removed. Difficulty is set by op, the number of operations: training uses op ≤ 15 (iGSM-med) or op ≤ 21 (iGSM-hard); out-of-distribution tests use op 20-23 or 28-32 (Sec. 2, p.5). The models use the GPT-2 architecture with rotary embeddings, mainly GPT2-12-12 (GPT-2-small size), pretrained from scratch on iGSM (arXiv App. D, p.19). Retry data: at each solution step, with probability "retry rate", a parameter that cannot yet be computed is inserted and followed by a [BACK] token, and this repeats geometrically (Sec. 4, p.8). Models are compared at equal numbers of training tokens (Sec. 4, p.8).
  - What it establishes:
    - Result 0, which the authors derive from the probing method of their Part 2.1. The models are pretrained on error-free data. After a model writes "Define [param] as" for a parameter that is not ready to compute, a probe on the last hidden layer detects the mistake about 60% of the time. The same probe is about 99% accurate over all parameters (Sec. 3.1, p.6; arXiv p.7). The most common mistake in iGSM is exactly this: naming a parameter that is not yet computable, i.e. skipping steps (Sec. 3, p.6).
    - "Retry upon regret" means regenerating whenever the probe fires. Even with a 99%-accurate detector, it beats beam search (beam 32) by only about 2 points at op=23 (iGSM-med) and op=32 (iGSM-hard) (Result 1, Sec. 3.2, p.7).
    - Pretraining on retry data beats pretraining on the same number of tokens of error-free data. Within a reasonable range, more inserted errors help more. Masking the erroneous tokens is unnecessary, and the trained models rarely retry at test time except at very high retry rates (Results 2-4, p.9). Adding retry data only at LoRA fine-tuning does not reproduce the gain. Full fine-tuning does, at a cost similar to continued pretraining (Result 7, arXiv Sec. 5, p.12; summarised in the ICLR version on p.4).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): iGSM-med, op=23, qp format: accuracy goes from 78% to 94% with retry rate 0.5 (Results 2-3, p.9; arXiv p.10). iGSM-med, op=23, pq format: retry upon regret gives 78% to 80%, while pretraining on retry data gives 78% to 95% (p.10; arXiv p.12). The probe detects about 60% of first mistakes, against 99% probing accuracy overall (Sec. 3.1, p.6).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): they do not claim that this synthetic data can directly help build future LLMs (p.4; Sec. 6, p.10). They study only logical reasoning, having removed arithmetic and common sense (Sec. 6, p.10). Follow-ups, such as rewriting real math data into this format, were not explored for lack of GPUs (Sec. 6, p.10). Sample complexity is not studied (arXiv App. D, p.19).
  - Relation to the review: bears on RQ2 and RQ3 as a controlled synthetic precedent. In iGSM the dominant error comes from one kind of decision, made at the start of a step (RQ2). The model's state registers the error just after it commits. Training on in-line corrections lets later tokens repair an earlier error inside the same answer, which bears on how errors propagate within an answer (RQ3).
  - The hinge: the errors are synthetic: inserted at random and always followed by a perfect correction, in small models trained from scratch on one data family. The review concerns the natural errors of pretrained LLMs.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "In a controlled study on synthetic grade-school math, models' internal states often signaled that a step was wrong, and pretraining on data that contains errors followed by immediate corrections raised accuracy more than training on error-free data."
  - Source passage: "often knows it has made a mistake, right after stating the parameter name" (Sec. 3.1, p.6). The same section gives about 60% detection on first mistakes and attributes the probe to Part 2.1. The retry-data results are Results 2-3 (p.9) and the Conclusion (Sec. 6, p.10), at equal training tokens (p.8).
  - Scope: the signal is read by a trained probe after the wrong parameter has been named. The probe is "version1": the model is frozen and a rank-8 update on the embedding layer is trained, which the authors describe as close to a linear classifier on the hidden states (pp.6-7). It flags about 60% of first mistakes; it does not foresee most errors. The accuracy gains are for pretraining from scratch on iGSM with GPT-2-small-size models. LoRA fine-tuning on retry data does not give them (Result 7).
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "In a controlled study with GPT-2-small-size models trained from scratch on synthetic grade-school math, a lightweight probe on the hidden states flagged about 60% of first mistakes just after the model had named a quantity it could not yet compute, and pretraining on solutions with inserted errors followed by immediate corrections gave higher accuracy than pretraining on the same number of tokens of error-free data, for example 94% versus 78% on the hardest out-of-distribution problems of one test family [ye2025physics22]."
- Bib notes: authors, title, pages 78136--78147 and year 2025 are from the proceedings.iclr.cc BibTeX export. The OpenReview search API's _bibtex agrees on authors, title and booktitle ("The Thirteenth International Conference on Learning Representations"). The note field records that the ICLR version is an extended abstract and gives the full version. The title is spelled as on the proceedings page ("Learn From Mistakes").

### shojaee2025illusion
- Source of record: https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/9b26ad15462c81548c0689188d2e8018-Abstract-Conference.html (NeurIPS 2025, Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 38, pp. 108018-108059 per the proceedings BibTeX, DOI 10.52202/085713-3603). The OpenReview search API lists forum YghiOusmvw as "NeurIPS 2025 poster". Access: full text (NeurIPS camera-ready https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/9b26ad15462c81548c0689188d2e8018-Paper-Conference.pdf; arXiv v3 https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06941v3, which arXiv marks as the camera-ready, and whose text matches in every passage used). Verdict on venue: NeurIPS 2025, not arXiv only.
- Retraction: none found. Crossref record 10.52202/085713-3603 has no update or relation field. It lists pp. 119605-119646, which is Curran's print pagination: the same 42-page length as the 108018-108059 in the NeurIPS BibTeX. Crossref also has a 2025 record titled "The Illusion of Thinking", by the same authors, in "SuperIntelligence - Robotics - Safety & Alignment" (DOI 10.70777/si.v2i6.15919). We do not use it. arXiv abs (v3) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: Apple. Four puzzles whose complexity is set by a size N: Tower of Hanoi, Checker Jumping, River Crossing and Blocks World (Sec. 3.1, pp.5-6). Thinking and non-thinking pairs: Claude 3.7 Sonnet with and without thinking, and DeepSeek-R1 versus V3 (Sec. 4.2.1, p.7). Reasoning models: o3-mini (medium and high), DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B and Claude-3.7-Sonnet with thinking (Sec. 4.2.2, p.7). Budgets were 64k tokens, with 25 samples per instance and complexity level (Sec. 4.1, p.6). Simulators check each move and whether the goal is reached; optimality is not graded (Sec. 3.1, p.5).
  - What it establishes:
    - Three regimes. At low complexity, non-thinking models match or beat thinking models; at medium complexity, thinking helps; at high complexity, both collapse (Sec. 4.2.1, p.7).
    - The accuracy of every reasoning model falls with complexity to near zero beyond a model-specific threshold. Thinking tokens first grow with complexity and then fall near the collapse point. The decline is strongest for o3-mini and weakest for Claude-3.7-Sonnet with thinking (Sec. 4.2.2, p.7; Fig. 6, p.8).
    - Giving the solution algorithm in the prompt did not improve Tower of Hanoi or Checker Jumping (Sec. 4.4, p.9; Fig. 8). The position of the first failure is non-monotonic in N: on Tower of Hanoi, failure comes before move 50 at N=12 but after more than 100 correct moves at N=10. It also differs across puzzles: about 100 correct moves in Tower of Hanoi against 4 in River Crossing (Sec. 4.4, pp.9-10; Fig. 9; Conclusion, p.10).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): minimum moves for n disks: 2^n − 1 (Sec. 3.1, p.5). Collapse begins at N=7-8, about 100-200 moves. The first failure typically comes within the first ~100 moves for N≈10 (about 10^3 moves needed) and around move 40 for N=8 (255 moves needed) (App. A.1, p.23). Budgets: 64k tokens (Sec. 4.1, p.6), with a maximum generation of 64,000 tokens (App. A.3, p.30).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): the Limitations section (p.10) names three points. The puzzles are a narrow slice of reasoning and may not capture real-world or knowledge-intensive reasoning. Most experiments use black-box API access to closed models, which rules out mechanistic analysis. Deterministic simulators assume that reasoning can be validated step by step, which may not transfer to open-ended domains. Elsewhere, the authors note that complexity cannot be compared across puzzles at the small N studied, so they track it within each puzzle; they attribute differences across puzzles to familiarity from training data (Sec. 4.4, p.10).
  - Requested records:
    - Growth of the move count with puzzle size: the minimum number of moves for n disks is 2^n − 1 (Sec. 3.1, p.5), and App. A.2.1 calls Tower of Hanoi exponentially scaling (p.24). The paper grades whether each move is valid and whether the target is reached, not whether the solution is optimal (Sec. 3.1, p.5).
    - Output-token limits: yes, the paper discusses them. Budgets are 64k tokens (Sec. 4.1, p.6; App. A.3, p.30). The abstract (p.1), Sec. 1 (p.3) and Sec. 4.2.2 (p.7) state that effort declines while models are still well below their generation-length limits. App. A.1, "Response to Main Criticisms" (p.23), argues that Tower of Hanoi failures are not context-limit failures: collapse begins at about 100-200 moves, and the first failure comes early in the sequence. It also reports that Tower of Hanoi instances with N>12 were removed. The same appendix answers two other critiques. On sampling, temperature-0 runs of R1 still collapse (p.23). On River Crossing, instances with N≥6 were excluded because the larger boat capacity changes the problem (p.23).
    - The authors' stated limitations are listed above.
  - Relation to the review: bears on how length-dependent failure should be read. Our reading, not the authors': Tower of Hanoi needs 2^N − 1 moves, each of which must be correct. That is the case in which the review's number of key decisions grows with length, so its own model expects exponential decay there. The non-monotonic and puzzle-dependent first-failure positions point to per-step risk that is not uniform (RQ2), and the fall in effort near collapse is a further reason not to model failure as token count times a constant rate. The authors explain the differences across puzzles by familiarity with the training data (p.10).
  - The hinge: each answer is a complete move sequence for a fully specified procedure, produced in one response and graded all-or-nothing. The review's key decisions are points of genuine choice within an answer.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "On puzzles whose difficulty can be raised step by step, such as Tower of Hanoi, the accuracy of reasoning models collapsed beyond a complexity threshold, and their reasoning effort first rose and then fell as complexity grew."
  - Source passage: "their reasoning effort increases with problem complexity up to a point, then declines" (Abstract, p.1). Collapse beyond a model-specific threshold: Sec. 4.2.2 (p.7) and Fig. 6 (p.8).
  - Scope: five reasoning models from early 2025 (o3-mini medium and high, DeepSeek-R1, R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, Claude-3.7-Sonnet with thinking) on four puzzles. Effort is measured in thinking tokens, within a 64k budget. The decline in effort is strongest for o3-mini and weakest for Claude.
  - Verdict: ACCURATE. Attribute the result to the tested models, and cite the NeurIPS version, whose App. A.1 answers the token-limit and sampling critiques, rather than v1.
- Bib notes: from the NeurIPS proceedings BibTeX export: authors, title, booktitle, volume (exported as "38, Main Conference"), pages 108018--108059, DOI, publisher Curran. The NeurIPS PDF byline reads "Keivan Alizadeh Vahid". The export writes "Alizadeh vahid"; the arXiv byline writes "Keivan Alizadeh"; and OpenReview writes "Seyed Iman Mirzadeh" for "Iman Mirzadeh". The bib follows the NeurIPS PDF byline. Crossref pages differ, as noted under Retraction.

### gema2025inverse
- Source of record: https://openreview.net/forum?id=NXgyHW1c7M (Transactions on Machine Learning Research, 12/2025). The OpenReview search API gives venue "Accepted by TMLR", venueid TMLR, with Featured Certification and J2C Certification. Access: full text (arXiv v2 https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.14417v2, the TMLR camera-ready, whose every page carries the running head "Published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research (12/2025)"). The arXiv journal-ref and comments confirm TMLR 12/2025 and the two certifications. The OpenReview PDF endpoint was behind a challenge page and was not opened. Verdict on venue: TMLR 2025, not arXiv only.
- Retraction: none found. The Crossref bibliographic query returns no record for this paper (TMLR has no DOIs), and the records it returns carry no relation fields. arXiv abs (v2) shows no withdrawal.
- Card:
  - Setting: authors from the Anthropic Fellows Program, Anthropic, Edinburgh and elsewhere. The tasks:
    - Simple counting with distractors: Misleading Math and Misleading Python, 2,500 questions each.
    - A Misleading Math variant framed as famous paradoxes, 812 questions.
    - Grades Regression with spurious features: 500 students, zero-shot and few-shot.
    - Zebra Puzzles from BBEH: 200 puzzles, grids from 5x5 to 8x8.
    - 15 advanced-AI-risk tasks from model-written evaluations.

- Card:
  - Setting: Towers of Hanoi with 20 disks, which takes 2^20 − 1 = 1,048,575 moves (Sec. 2.3, p.4; Sec. 4, p.8; Fig. 8, p.11). The name MAKER stands for its three parts (Sec. 1, p.2; Sec. 3, pp.4-8):
    - Maximal Agentic decomposition: one move per agent call. Each agent sees the current state, the previous move and the overall strategy.
    - First-to-ahead-by-k voting: samples are drawn until one candidate leads every other by k votes (Alg. 2, p.4).
    - Red-flagging: overly long or incorrectly formatted responses are discarded.

    Because the overall strategy is in every prompt, the agents execute a plan rather than devise one (Sec. 4.1, p.9). Final run: gpt-4.1-mini with k=3, the first vote at temperature 0 and later votes at 0.1, a 750-token cap on output and a red-flagging parser (Sec. 4.4, p.10).
  - What it establishes:
    - Theory assuming independent, identically distributed per-step errors (Eqs. 9-18, pp.5-7). In the worst-case analysis, where the correct answer races a single alternative, voting converges to the correct answer only if per-step success p exceeds 0.5 (Eq. 9, p.5). The informal condition is that no incorrect answer is more likely than the correct one (Sec. 2.1, p.3). With m steps per subtask, success on the whole task is (1 + ((1−p)/p)^k)^(−s/m). The k needed grows as Θ(ln s). With m=1 the expected cost grows as Θ(s ln s), and it grows exponentially in m.
    - Estimated per-step error rates stayed flat as the number of disks grew. Small non-reasoning models had error rates comparable to reasoning models (Sec. 4.2, p.9; Fig. 6a, p.10).
    - The full 20-disk run finished with zero errors (Sec. 4.4, p.10). After the first k rounds, the number of undecided steps fell exponentially; one pathological step needed 18 rounds (Fig. 8, p.11). The weaker first-to-k voting rule also solved the task (p.11).
    - Correlated errors. With long outputs allowed, collisions (steps whose first two votes were both wrong) far exceeded the one or two expected under independence, especially with a repairing parser, and red-flagging reduced them (Sec. 4.5, pp.11-12; Fig. 9b). Separately, two independent runs over 10K random steps shared no erroneous step (Sec. 4.3, p.10).
  - Headline numbers (verbatim, with location): for gpt-4.1-mini at τ=0.1, the estimated per-step error 1−p is .0022, k_min is 3 and the projected cost is $3.5K, at a target success probability of 0.95 (Fig. 6b, p.10). These are pre-run estimates. The error rate rises sharply once responses pass about 700 tokens (Fig. 9a and Sec. 4.5, p.11). Undecided steps start from 1048575 in Fig. 8 (p.11).
  - Authors' own stated limitations or scope (paraphrase plus location): Sec. 5 (pp.12-14) names several. The paper covers execution of a given strategy, and extending it to steps that need insight is future work. The theory assumes errors are independent and identically distributed across steps, yet a few steps had much higher error rates, and the authors call handling correlated errors an open problem. All agents used the same LLM. The method assumes the task can be broken into small, simple steps, and which tasks resist such decomposition is unknown. Sec. 2.1 (p.3) adds that the definition of a step is assumed to be given.
  - Relation to the review: a direct precedent for the review's analysis of voting at a decision. In its worst-case, two-candidate analysis, MAKER needs per-step success above one half for voting to converge (Eq. 9, p.5; Sec. 4.3, p.9), which matches the review's condition e_key < 1/2. Its own data show correlated errors at particular steps that the independent-error theory does not capture (Fig. 9b; Sec. 5, p.13). It also shows exponential decay in steps (Eq. 8, p.5) being offset by error correction at each step.
  - The hinge: every step is a uniform, fully specified execution step: the strategy is in the prompt, and votes must match exactly on the move and the next state. The review's key decisions are points of genuine choice, where errors may be shared across samples.
- Use 1 — intended claim: "Decomposing a task into minimal subtasks and voting at each step, while discarding malformed outputs to reduce correlated errors, completed a task of more than one million LLM steps with zero errors."
  - Source passage: "With these calibrations, the full system solved the problem perfectly." (Sec. 4.4, p.10). The components and the red-flag criteria are in Sec. 3.3 (pp.7-8) and Sec. 4.4 (p.10). The effect of red-flagging on correlated errors is in Sec. 4.5 (pp.11-12).
  - Scope: Towers of Hanoi with 20 disks (1,048,575 moves); gpt-4.1-mini; first-to-ahead-by-3 voting; red flags for overlong outputs (over 750 tokens) or misformatted ones; the overall solution strategy in every prompt; one reported run; an arXiv preprint.
  - Verdict: NARROW → safe wording: "MAKER split 20-disk Towers of Hanoi into its 2^20 − 1 (about 1.05 million) single-move subtasks, solved each with independent gpt-4.1-mini calls whose prompt included the solution strategy, chose each move by first-to-ahead-by-3 voting, and discarded overlong or misformatted outputs to reduce correlated errors; the reported run completed every move with zero errors [meyerson2025maker]."
