Every classification in this survey is one rater's reading. A single rater scored all 256 systems, rounded ambiguous cases down, and used half-scores only when one criterion was clearly met and another partly met. No inter-rater agreement is reported, and independent re-rating remains future work. To make the reading checkable rather than merely asserted, each row of the released table carries its source and a one-line justification (and, for the 126 most recently added systems, its evidence tier), so a reader who disagrees with a score can locate the disagreement.

The evidence is public, and public evidence is uneven. It ranges from peer-reviewed studies and open-source artifacts to vendor claims. A production claim counts only when it names a benchmark or integration target, a blog or interview counts only when stronger evidence corroborates it, and production claims are not pooled with peer-reviewed evidence in corpus-level statistics. The classifications describe what systems document, not a complete inventory of private production behavior; a deployment that runs a governed loop without describing it would be missed.

The corpus has a cutoff and a selection rule. It covers January 2023 to October 2026, and systems released after the cutoff are not read. Including systems that are publicly described as self-improving, scaffolded, or agentic deliberately admits a contrast set of 52, so proportions over the whole corpus mix adapting systems with systems admitted for comparison. The corpus was also built in two passes, and gate types, feedback classes, and governance properties are explicit columns only for the 126 most recently added systems. Counts over those columns, such as the 89 of the 96 core systems with a local loop that close it by program, describe those systems and are not gate counts over all 256.

The survey runs no experiments. Its findings are structural readings of published methods and documentation, and its feedback-class predictions are expectations checked against where systems sit, not causal tests. The error-structure hypothesis discussed in Section REF(sec:discussion), that deployment failures concentrate in a slowly growing catalogue of recurring modes, is not tested here; the findings about gates, feedback, and promotion do not depend on it.
